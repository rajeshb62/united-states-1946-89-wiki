Covert Action (Church Committee Hearings, Volume 7)

Author / body: U.S. Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities (Frank Church, Chairman; John G. Tower, Vice Chairman)
Published: 1975 — U.S. Government Printing Office, Washington (hearings 4–5 December 1975; Chile staff report dated 18 December 1975 in appendix)

Overview

Volume 7 publishes the Select Committee’s public covert action hearings and, as a major appendix, the staff report Covert Action in Chile: 1963–1973. Hearing days examine the purposes, authorization, and limits of U.S. covert action through witnesses including former U.S. Ambassador to Chile Edward M. Korry and a policy panel featuring Clark M. Clifford (counsel to President Truman; early National Security Act framer), Cyrus Vance, and other named participants. The Chile staff report then lays out a continuous narrative of CIA and U.S. covert programs in Chile from the 1964 election through the Allende years and the 1973 coup context, with sections on 40 Committee control, intelligence estimates, and congressional oversight. Further appendices reprint core statutes (National Security Act of 1947 as amended, War Powers Resolution of 1973, and Hughes–Ryan-related foreign-assistance amendment text as printed).

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the volume is a primary source on Cold War covert action policy and the Chile case as Congress presented them in December 1975: election support and “spoiling” campaigns; Track themes and the Schneider crisis as they appear in the staff report; multinational-corporation issues; and the statutory overlay from 1947 through Hughes–Ryan. Pair with the November 1975 assassination interim report (Schneider/Diem/Trujillo/Cuba/Congo) and Book I Part VIII (Covert Action) for the full 1975–76 Committee picture.

OCR note: continuous IA _djvu.txt through statutory appendices; spacing and character noise typical of GPO scans. Hearing openers, Chile staff-report title page, and appendix letters located.

Narrative Source Flow

Hearing day — Thursday, December 4, 1975

Opens public covert-action hearings. Committee framing of covert action under the National Security Act and NSC/40 Committee system. Principal early testimony includes Edward M. Korry, former U.S. Ambassador to Chile, on embassy–CIA relations, knowledge of covert programs, and Chile policy across the late 1960s–1970 transition as he presents it. Questioning develops ambassadorial authority versus clandestine service channels and the problem of secret wars alongside declared diplomacy.

Hearing day — Friday, December 5, 1975

Policy and historical panel. Clark M. Clifford (introduced as counsel to President Truman and a framer figure for the 1947 National Security Act and National Security Council) offers an opening statement and testimony on original intent for CIA peacetime clandestine missions and later expansion of covert action. Cyrus Vance and other panelists (as named in the transcript, including figures grouped with Clifford and Vance in the day’s examination) address limits, authorization, and whether covert action should continue under stricter controls. The day is the Committee’s set-piece public debate on covert action’s future, complementary to Book I’s later findings.

Appendix A — Staff Report: Covert Action in Chile: 1963–1973 (Committee Print, December 18, 1975)

Full staff report printed for committee use and included in the hearings volume. Front note states that after the December 4 hearing the Committee received additional information affecting sections on multinational corporations, intelligence estimates and covert action, and congressional oversight, to be reflected in the Final Report. Preface: facts true to staff’s best ability; judgments tentative; purpose to lay out basic facts for public hearings; based on CIA, State, Defense, and NSC documents and testimony; Chilean names largely omitted to protect sources/methods and limit harm.

Staff report contents (as printed)

  • I. Overview and Background — Overview of covert action in Chile; issues; historical background of recent U.S.–Chilean relations.
  • II. The Range of Covert Action in Chile — Covert action and other clandestine activities; techniques; covert action and multinational corporations.
  • III. Major Covert Action Programs and Their Effects — 1964 presidential election; covert action 1964–1969; 1970 election “spoiling” campaign; covert action between 4 September and 24 October 1970; covert action during the Allende years 1970–1973; post-1973.
  • IV. Chile: Authorization, Assessment, and Oversight — 40 Committee authorization and control 1969–1973; intelligence estimates and covert action; congressional oversight.
  • V. Preliminary Conclusions — Covert action and U.S. foreign policy; executive command and control; role of Congress; intelligence judgments versus covert operations; major programs.
  • Appendix (to staff report). Chronology: Chile 1962–1975

Staff report headline facts (as stated in overview)

The report’s opening overview states extensive, continuous covert U.S. involvement in Chile 1963–1973; about three million dollars covertly to influence the 1964 Chilean presidential election; about eight million dollars covertly between 1970 and the September 1973 military coup (over three million in fiscal 1972 alone). It distinguishes covert action from clandestine collection and liaison; describes techniques from propaganda placement to large-scale party support and coup-related efforts; recounts a 1970 effort at President Nixon’s express request, with injunctions not to inform State, Defense, or the Ambassador, and without informing the 40 Committee, including passage of weapons to Chilean officers in a coup-related context tied to the Schneider kidnapping (weapons returned; group that killed Schneider described as apparently not the same as the group that received CIA weapons). On direct involvement in the 1973 coup, the Committee staff report states it found no evidence of direct U.S. covert involvement in that coup, while noting 1970 coup-fomenting efforts, a post-1970 policy of opposition to Allende (overt and covert), and continued intelligence contact with the Chilean military including officers participating in coup plotting. Allegations on truckers’ strikes and related claims are treated with qualifications as printed.
