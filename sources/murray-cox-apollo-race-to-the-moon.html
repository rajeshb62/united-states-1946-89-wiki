Period-Overlap Note

This source belongs entirely in United States 1946–89 (Space Task Group 1958 through Apollo 11/13 and early-1970s program close). No copy to 1866–1919, 1920–45, or 1990–present.

Overview

Apollo: The Race to the Moon is a narrative history of how the American manned lunar program was engineered, managed, and flown, told through the people who stayed on the ground. Murray and Cox (interview-based, with extensive NASA and contractor access) follow the Space Task Group from its 1958 origins through Kennedy’s 1961 commitment, the mode and hardware decisions (L.O.R., Saturn V, command/service module, lunar module), the Apollo 1 fire and recovery, Mueller’s all-up testing, Mission Control’s evolution, and the flights through Apollo 11, 12, and 13. The book explicitly is not astronaut biography and not a comprehensive institutional history—it is an “epic captured in miniature” through engineers, managers, and flight controllers (Gilruth, Faget, Shea, Low, Petrone, Kraft, Kranz, Lunney, Houbolt, and dozens of trench-level figures).

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the entire narrative sits inside the period: Space Task Group formation (1958), Kennedy decision (1961), fire and rebuild (1967), Saturn V and Apollo 8 (1968), landing (1969), and Apollo 13 (1970), with epilogue and program-close material through the early 1970s. Use beside astronaut memoir sources (e.g. Built to Last) for the ground-operations complement; no copy needed to 1866–1919, 1920–45, or 1990–present.

EPUB structure: review blurbs; title/copyright (1989 / 2004 / 2010); Foreword to the 2004 Edition; Acknowledgments; Apollo Abbreviations; Prologue; Book I — Gathering (Chs. 1–5); Book II — Building (Chs. 6–17); Book III — Flying (Chs. 18–30); Epilogue; Notes; glossary/index material in back matter.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter / blurbs / title

Lines 1–62. Contemporary review quotes (Collins, Sheffield, Petit, Mallon, etc.); title page; copyright blocks (© 1989 Cox and Murray, Inc.; 2004 South Mountain Books; 2010 ebook); ISBN 0-976008-0-6; dedication to Apollo workers and families.

Foreword to the 2004 Edition

Lines 63–155. Authors on modest 1989 sales but strong reception among Apollo veterans; republication with cosmetic changes only; deliberate anachronisms (CRT Mission Control, computer-capacity comparisons); Apollo 13 film vs. real displays; argument that Apollo was engineered excellence, not impetuous gamble; timeline from Space Task Group (Nov 5, 1958, forty-five people) to Apollo 11 (July 20, 1969).

Acknowledgments

Lines 156–254. Jack Trombka and agent Amanda Urban as origin; Simon & Schuster editors (Mayhew, Shipley); Robert Sherrod archive; NASA center public-affairs and history offices; interview contributors; long list of “unsung” helpers; Apollo Abbreviations note on insider pronunciation and period rules (N.A.C.A., L.O.R., NASA, DPS).

Prologue. May 25, 1961

Lines 255–447. Kennedy’s joint-session speech framed through Washington spring 1961 and Cape weather; reactions at Hangar S and in transit (Petrone, Preston, Simpkinson, Beddingfield, Koos, Gilruth “aghast”); book’s scope—ground workers who put men on the moon and brought them back, not the astronauts themselves.

Chapter 1 — “That famous Space Task Group is akin to the Mayflower”

Lines 448–1083. Owen Maynard’s April 1959 drive from Canada to Hampton/Langley; Peninsula isolation and STG culture; Gilruth’s forty-five-person shop; early engineer migrations; Max Faget already thinking beyond Mercury toward the moon.

Chapter 2 — “I could picture the astronauts looking down at it with binoculars”

Lines 1084–1420. Max Faget’s 1959 Dolley Madison House “blue-sky” seminar with Glennan, von Braun, Pickering, Silverstein; early lunar-landing design thinking; Silverstein ordering moon references stripped from George Low’s draft long-range plan.

Chapter 3 — “Those days were out of the Dark Ages”

Lines 1421–1936. First STG Cape contingent (June 1959) and Big Joe boilerplate heat-shield test; Hangar S rope-off workspace, sixteen-hour days, ammonia against mosquitoes; Air Force treating STG as low-priority amateurs; improvised Cape operations before modern clean-room culture.

Chapter 4 — “He would rather not have done it”

Lines 1937–2590. Bob Seamans’s gloomy fall 1960—Eisenhower budget cuts, Saturn second-stage casualty, Mercury-only future; Kennedy election and campaign “space gap” rhetoric; Kennedy’s pre-Gagarin ambivalence about manned spaceflight; Gilruth/Faget/Petrone later veneration vs. Seamans’s contemporary reading of presidential indifference.

Chapter 5 — “We’re going to the moon”

Lines 2591–2985. Kennedy’s April 1961 apogee shattered by Gagarin (Apr 12); political shock and congressional pressure; Wiesner/Webb/Johnson deliberations; path to May 25 speech and the decade deadline—wiki hinge for Kennedy commitment.

Chapter 6 — “The flight article has got to dominate”

Lines 2986–3525. Rocco Petrone’s summer 1961 Cape pivot—Heavy Space Vehicle Systems Office, site selection, mobile launcher and V.A.B. logic; “flight article dominates” as Cape design principle; constraints (explosive potential, downrange safety, labor pool, weather) shaping Kennedy Space Center expansion.

Chapter 7 — “We had more harebrained schemes than you could shake a stick at”

Lines 3526–4064. Gilruth on Kennedy’s word “safely”; reliability-number fights (Johnson, Piland, Faget, Gilruth, Walt Williams); direct ascent, earth-orbit rendezvous, lunar crasher, and other mode sketches before L.O.R. crystallized.

Chapter 8 — “Somewhat as a voice in the wilderness…”

Lines 4065–4508. Lunar-orbit rendezvous origins—Tom Dolan/Vought modular lander, Langley lunar-parking-orbit work, John Houbolt’s persistent lobbying to Abe Seamans; energy-budget logic and rendezvous risk as the central engineering controversy.

Chapter 9 — “What sonofabitch thinks it isn’t the right thing to do?”

Lines 4509–5356. Joe Shea’s January 1962 arrival; Langley mode briefings and Shea’s conversion to L.O.R.; hidden STG rethinking vs. public anti-rendezvous posture; Shea named ASPO chief; North American (C.S.M.) and Grumman (L.E.M.) prime contracts—eighteen months after Kennedy speech, NASA knew how it would go.

Chapter 10 — “It aged me, I’m sure”

Lines 5357–5777. F-1 engine combustion instability—June 28, 1962 test-stand destruction at Edwards; Rocketdyne/Marshall struggle to tame the 1.5-million-pound-thrust engine that made single-launch L.O.R. possible.

Chapter 11 — “It sounded reckless”

Lines 5778–6186. George Mueller’s all-up testing plan vs. German and N.A.C.A. incremental flight-test cultures; Shea’s “three tribes” (Peenemünde Germans, N.A.C.A. craftsmen, I.C.B.M. systems engineers); Mueller hired to impose schedule discipline across centers.

Chapter 12 — “Hey, it isn’t that complicated”

Lines 6187–6729. Shea in Houston (Nov 1963) finds no L.O.R.-compatible C.S.M. design; North American contract award controversy—Martin ranked first, North American selected after rescoring toward experimental-aircraft experience; Stormy Storms and the C.S.M. program’s troubled start.

Chapter 13 — “We want you to go fix it”

Lines 6730–7097. F-1 injector redesign finally tames combustion instability (Castenholz, Thomson); five-engine cluster test (Apr 1965); V.A.B. High Bay and crawler-transporter milestones; Shea’s accumulating stress as ASPO ramrod.

Chapter 14 — “Did he say ‘fire’?”

Lines 7098–7772. Apollo 1 (AS-204) pad fire, Jan 27, 1967—Before the Fire / After the Fire divide; Simpkinson’s reconstruction (scuffed cable, glycol fumes, pure-oxygen environment, flammable netting); Grissom, White, Chaffee; program history bisected by the catastrophe.

Chapter 15 — The Crucible

Lines 7773–8615. Leadership converges at K.S.C. after the fire; Shea moves into astronauts’ quarters; investigative panel (Borman over Schirra); Block II redesign, hatch, wiring, flammables; North American–NASA confrontation and the program’s forced maturation.

Chapter 16 — “You’ve got to start biting somewhere”

Lines 8616–9036. Shea’s breakdown and departure; George Low recruited to head ASPO (Mathews demurs; Webb “persuasion” charade); Low as “knitter of people” vs. Shea’s impatient charisma; recovery management through first post-fire flights.

Chapter 17 — “And then on launch day it worked”

Lines 9037–9630. Saturn V stages arriving at the Cape; stacking in the V.A.B.; Apollo 4 (AS-501) as Mueller’s first all-up test—November 9, 1967; Rigell/Petrone scale from Redstone hand-holding to million-pound-thrust operations.

Chapter 18 — “We’re going to put a guy in that thing and light it”

Lines 9631–10302. Manned Spacecraft Center’s deceptively bland campus; Flight Operations’ evolution from Mercury flight-test mindset toward real-time ground control; Glynn Lunney and the Operations Division inventing their role as the spacecraft circles the earth.

Chapter 19 — “There will always be people who want to work in that room”

Lines 10303–10802. Building 30 and the MOCR—Staff Support Rooms, SPAN, recovery coordination; Gemini shift to Houston control; CRT consoles, simulations, and the culture of controllers who endure headaches to work “in that room.”

Chapter 20 — “The flight director may take any necessary action”

Lines 10803–11153. Flight director’s sweeping mission-rule authority; Chris Kraft as model (piccolo theory vs. controllers’ awe at his technical depth); Gene Kranz and the flight-director corps; power, anonymity, and exposure.

Chapter 21 — “There was no mercy in those days”

Lines 11154–11851. Bill Tindall appointed “Chief of Apollo Data Priority Coordination” (Aug 1967)—Mission Techniques; who-does-what fights between astronauts, contractors, and controllers; Tindall meetings and simulation brutality as Apollo learns to fly its machines.

Chapter 22 — “You’ve lost the engines?”

Lines 11852–12327. Apollo 6 (Apr 4, 1968)—severe pogo and multiple S-II engine shutdowns amid national upheaval; Apollo engineers largely oblivious to Vietnam and politics; performance problems that feed George Low’s Apollo 8 gamble.

Chapter 23 — “It was darn scary”

Lines 12328–12915. George Low’s August 1968 proposal to send Apollo 8 circumlunar; Kraft, Bostick, Phillips, Paine, Mueller, Webb deliberations; compressing MPAD calculations and selling “C-prime” as schedule rescue and Soviet-race countermove.

Chapter 24 — “We … we’re go on that, Flight”

Lines 12916–13829. Lunar module as novel machine—Grumman’s flimsy materials, volatile propellants, hand-milled fittings; Apollo 9 L.E.M. checkout in earth orbit; rendezvous and docked DPS burns as prerequisites for lunar landing.

Chapter 25 — “Well, let’s light this sumbitch and it better work”

Lines 13830–14428. Apollo 11 landing night—E.V.A., Armstrong’s “one small step”; post-landing anomalies (1201/1202 alarms, switch breakage, engine-lift-off sensor) worked through MOCR, SPAN, MER, and contractor rooms while the world watched.

Chapter 26 — “I think we need to do a little more all-weather testing”

Lines 14429–15087. Apollo 12 launch (Nov 14, 1969)—Kapryan’s weather gamble, lightning strike twice during ascent, SCE-to-Aux workaround; Griffin’s first launch shift; Conrad’s exuberance and the program’s continuing anomaly culture.

Chapter 27 — “You really need to understand that the C.S.M. is dying”

Lines 15088–15972. Apollo 13 (Apr 1970)—Swigert replaces Mattingly; cryo stir and O2 Tank 2 explosion; Sy Liebergot’s EECOM console; Kranz’s White Team crisis opening; Glynn Lunney’s assessment of Flight Operations’ “crowning achievement.”

Chapter 28 — “You don’t have time to worry”

Lines 15973–16640. Lunney’s Black Team shift—power-down decisions, L.E.M. activation, consumables and navigation; Kranz/Tindall/MER coordination; improvised procedures under time pressure as Odyssey dies and Aquarius becomes lifeboat.

Chapter 29 — “I hope the guys who thought this up knew what they were doing”

Lines 16641–17596. L.E.M. lifeboat concept from Grumman specs (Kelly/Munier, 1961) through simulations; consumables math, carbon-dioxide scrubber hack, cold and sleep deprivation; re-entry after service-module jettison—Thirteen preserved, not yet saved.

Chapter 30 — “We drank the wine at the pace they handed it to us”

Lines 17597–18055. Kennedy’s Apollo as national boast with a deadline; Apollo 13 as last moment of public transfixion; fading space enthusiasm, spin-off defensiveness, and NASA bureaucratization; remaining flights (14–17) and program wind-down through 1972.

Epilogue. Twenty Years after the First Landing

Lines 18056–18689. 1989 retrospectives—Aaron’s “dominoes” uniqueness, family sacrifices (Cape/Houston divorce rates), gentlemen-engineers culture, aging veterans (Webb, Gilruth, Kraft, Houbolt, Petrone, etc.); Petrone on unreported “Renaissance” meaning; what Apollo symbolized vs. what the public remembers.

Notes

Lines 18690–19859. Endnotes tied to interview sources and technical references; back-matter book divisions and glossary/index repeats.

Coverage Guide

Space Task Group origins and Cape “Dark Ages” (wiki early manned space)

Chs. 1–3 + Prologue reactions: Langley/Hampton migration, Faget/Gilruth shop, Hangar S improvisations—foundation for 1958–61 NASA manned-flight pages.

Kennedy decision and political economy of commitment

Chs. 4–5: Eisenhower retrenchment, Seamans’s budget fears, Gagarin shock, May 25 1961 speech—pair with diplomatic histories for Cold War competition frame.

Mode, hardware, and center politics (wiki engineering core)

Chs. 6–13: L.O.R. (Houbolt/Shea), Saturn V/F-1, Mueller all-up, North American C.S.M. contract, Grumman L.E.M.—primary source for how Apollo was designed, not just flown.

Fire, recovery, and ASPO leadership transition

Chs. 14–16: Apollo 1 crucible, Block II, Shea→Low at ASPO—essential for 1967 hinge and contractor–NASA relationship pages.

Saturn V, Cape operations, and all-up testing

Chs. 17 + 6 + 10–11: V.A.B., crawler, F-1 instability, Apollo 4—launch-vehicle and K.S.C. infrastructure material.

Mission Control and flight operations (wiki operations spine)

Chs. 18–21 + 19–20: MOCR evolution, flight directors, Tindall Mission Techniques, simulations—densest ground-ops narrative in the period set.

Flight sequence: 6 → 8 → 9 → 11 → 12 → 13

Chs. 22–29: unmanned failures enabling Apollo 8 gamble; L.E.M. checkout; landing anomalies; lightning strike; Thirteen lifeboat—event-level mining for mission pages.

Program close and cultural aftermath

Ch. 30 + Epilogue: public attention fade, bureaucracy, family cost, veteran memory—use for late-Apollo/early-1970s cultural pages; Epilogue’s 1989 coda is period-gated spill for reflection only.
