Overview

Chip War is Chris Miller’s synthetic history of semiconductors as strategic technology: from the transistor and integrated circuit through Cold War military and NASA demand, Fairchild and Intel, Japanese memory competition, Korean and Taiwanese manufacturing ascent, personal-computer and fabless shifts, and twenty-first-century U.S.–China rivalry over advanced logic and lithography. Miller writes in short chapters grouped into parts, mixing firm-level stories with state policy and supply-chain geography.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki, period-gate hard: prioritize Introduction and early parts on U.S. invention, Pentagon/offset demand, Silicon Valley founding firms, Soviet copying limits, Japan’s 1980s challenge, and the beginnings of offshore assembly and Taiwan’s foundry path as they appear through 1989. Later parts on 1990s–2020s globalization, China’s rise, ASML EUV, and export controls are continuity/spill only—do not treat them as primary evidence for 1946–89 without clear backward linkage. The book’s presentist frame is external to the wiki’s contemporary lens; mine what actors did and said in-period.

Chapter 2 — The Switch

Lines 709–813. CHAPTER 2 The Switch William Shockley had long assumed that if a better “switch” was to be found, it would be with the help of a type of material called semiconductors. Shockley, who’d been born in London to a globe-trotting mining engineer, had grown up amid the fruit trees of the sleepy California town of Palo Alto. An only child, he was utterly convinced of his superiority over anyone around him-and he let everyone know it. He went to college at Caltech, in Southern California, before completing a PhD in physics at MIT and starting work at Bell Labs in New Jersey, which at the time was one of the world’s leading centers of science and engineering. He hypothesized that placing a piece of semiconductor material like silicon in the presence of an electric field could attract “free electrons” stored inside to cluster near the edge of the semiconductor. If enough electrons were attracted by the electric field, the edge of the semiconductor would be transformed into a conductive material, like a metal, which always has large numbers of free electrons.

Coverage Guide

Cold War origins and U.S. invention (priority)

Introduction + early chapters (steel-to-silicon, switch, Noyce/Kilby IC, liftoff, military mass production, Fairchild culture): 1940s–60s U.S. semiconductor origins and defense pull.

American system and Soviet comparison (priority)

Soviet Silicon Valley / copy-it chapters; transistor diplomacy; women assembly labor; precision strike and Pentagon offset: Cold War political economy of chips through the 1970s–80s.

Japan shock and U.S. response (priority through 1980s)

Memory competition, trade frictions, and U.S. industry/government responses in the 1980s—core for late-period industrial policy and firm strategy (Intel pivot narratives as they fall in-period).

Taiwan/Korea foundations (period edge)

Early TSMC/foundry and Korean chaebol entries that begin before 1990: usable for late-1980s supply-chain geography; full foundry globalization is mostly later.

Post-1989 spill (continuity only)

Parts on 1990s fabless model maturation, China’s catch-up, advanced lithography chokepoints, and contemporary export controls: not primary 1946–89 evidence.
