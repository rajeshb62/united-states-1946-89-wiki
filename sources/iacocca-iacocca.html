Overview

Iacocca is Lee Iacocca’s autobiography of a thirty-eight-year automotive career, written with William Novak after the Chrysler rescue made him a national figure. The narrative opens on the July 13, 1978 firing as Ford president and the warehouse exile on his fifty-fourth birthday, then returns to immigrant family origins in Allentown, engineering and sales training at Ford, the Mustang and product campaigns, the presidency under Henry Ford II, dismissal, and the near-bankruptcy and recovery of Chrysler through federal loan guarantees, management overhaul, labor concessions, the K-car, and the minivan. Paperback materials and “straight talk” chapters address Japan competition, labor-management, and public service themes of the early 1980s.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book is a primary business memoir of Detroit’s postwar auto order: Ford’s River Rouge training world of 1946, dealer and marketing power in the 1950s–70s, executive culture under Henry Ford II, the 1979–82 Chrysler crisis and federal industrial rescue, UAW bargaining, and the early-1980s product recovery. Childhood and Depression chapters and any post-1984 paperback reflections are kept for continuity; the industrial core sits inside the wiki period.

I — The Family

Lines 459–858. THE FAMILY Nicola Iacocca, my father, arrived in this country in 1902 at the age of twelve-poor, alone, and scared. He used to say the only thing he was sure of when he got here was that the world was round. (That’s thinly-sliced steak with melted cheese on an Italian roll.) My father set it up, and I put in the money. Now when I look back on my father, I only remember a man of great vigor and boundless energy. Once I was in Palm Springs for a Ford dealer meeting and I invited my father to come out for a brief vacation.

My father had taught me this fact of life thirty years earlier, but I guess I wasn’t listening. I sure saw the light in November of 1981! XXIII — Public Man, Public Office

Lines 11359–11827. PUBLIC MAN, PUBLIC OFFICE By the middle of 1983, when the company was solidly on its feet again, there were stories floating around that I was running for President. I guess the rumors started because of all the TV commercials I did for Chrysler. Many people now think I’m an actor. But that’s ridiculous. Everybody knows that being an actor doesn’t qualify you to be President! During the congressional debate, the ads we ran to explain our position were all signed by me. The campaign was highly effective, and when it was over, our ad agency decided to take the idea of my accountability one step further by featuring my face in television commercials. We were already perceived as being very different from the rest of the American auto industry. In marketing terms, the choice we faced was simple-either we could try to join the crowd and become one of the boys, or we could accept our separate identity and try to make it work to our advantage. By featuring the chairman in our ads, we chose the second course. A guy will write in longhand: “Why don’t you turn this country around? Why are you wasting your time selling cars?” People are hungry to be led. I don’t believe for a moment that we live in an antihero society. It’s just that since Eisenhower, we haven’t found ourselves a leader we can depend on.

XXIV — A Bittersweet Victory

Lines 11828–12366. A BITTERSWEET VICTORY In 1982, when the smoke of battle finally cleared, good things began to happen. Only three years earlier, the Chrysler Corporation had to sell 2.3 million cars and trucks just to break even. Unfortunately, we were only selling around 1 million. With a little quick math, you can understand how that doesn’t add up. But now, through the combined effort of a lot of different people, we had reduced our break-even point all the way down to 1.1 million units. Before long we were actually adding people to our payroll and signing up new dealers. “The Mustang was an experiment. The Mark III was an experiment. This car is another winner.” But Henry wouldn’t buy it. In my book, if you’re not number one, then you’ve got to innovate. If you’re Ford, you’ve got to beat GM to the punch. You’ve got to find market niches that they haven’t even thought of. Hard work is essential. But there’s also a time for rest and relaxation, for going to see your kid in the school play or at a swim meet. And if you don’t do those things while the kids are young, there’s no way to make it up later on.

XXV — How to Save Lives on the Road

Lines 12371–12788. HOW TO SAVE LIVES ON THE ROAD On the whole, we Americans are good drivers. And compared to drivers in other countries, we’re terrific. Although far too many people are killed each year on roads and highways, our traffic-death rate of 3.15 per 100 million vehicle miles is the lowest in the world. I don’t pretend to be an expert on driving. But I do know a few things about cars. And I want to explain why seat belts-and not air bags-are the key to reducing traffic fatalities in the United States. For years I’ve been promoting a very unpopular cause: mandatory seat-belt use. That’s because seat belts are even more effective on the ground than in the air. If you violate that law, the airline has the right to throw you off the flight. Originally, seat belts in cars were used only for racing. When both Ford and Chrysler offered seat belts in their 1956 models, there were few takers. As one GM official said at the time, “We would have been better off selling the bags and giving away the cars.” I suspect that ten years after this book is published, the government will still be debating air bags. When the crusaders get on their high horses, it’s impossible to stop them.

Back in 1971, Chrysler had purchased 15 percent of Mitsubishi and arranged to import some of their excellent small cars under the Dodge and Chrysler names. We’ve been partners ever since. The talks were held in the shrine city of Kyoto. During one of the breaks, I went out for a walk with Dr. Tornio Kubo, the dynamic chairman of Mitsubishi. Whenever this subject comes up, the Japanese always say it’s not the yen that’s too weak but the dollar that’s too strong. There’s certainly some truth to that charge, and our recent fiscal policies haven’t helped. The Reagan administration has to accept some of the blame, because its policy of tight money and high interest rates has made our dollar too attractive to foreign capital. But as we continue to fall behind Japan, it’s become increasingly clear that there’s also such a thing as too little. We have to take action. We must replace free trade with fair trade. If Japan-or any other nation-protects its markets, we should be doing the same.

Epilogue: The Great Lady

Lines 14319–end. THE GREAT LADY When President Reagan asked me to serve as chairman of the Statue of Liberty-Ellis Island Centennial Commission, I was up to my ears at Chrysler. But I accepted anyway. People asked me: “Why did you take this on? Don’t you have enough to do?” But this was a labor of love for my mother and father, who used to tell me about Ellis Island. My parents were greenhorns. They didn’t know the language. They didn’t know what to do when they came here. They were poor, and they had nothing. The island was part of my being-not the place itself, but what it stood for and how tough an experience it was. They’re asking themselves: “Will we be as good as our parents in coping with these new changes, or will we be left out in the cold?” And our kids are beginning to ask: “Do we have to lower our expectations and our standard of living?” Well, I want to say to them: It doesn’t have to be that way.

Coverage Guide

Immigrant family and path into Ford (to 1946+)

Family, school, and early chapters: Nicola and Antoinette Iacocca, Allentown, ethnic prejudice, education, and entry into Ford’s 1946 student engineer loop at the Rouge—baseline for postwar mobility into Big Three management.

Ford product, sales, and presidency

Ford Story chapters: district sales, the 1956 safety campaign, Mustang, product planning, Mark III, and rise to the presidency; relationship with Henry Ford II; board and palace politics culminating in the 1978 firing.

Chrysler crisis and federal rescue

Chrysler Story chapters: joining a failing company, inventory and quality disasters, loan-guarantee politics in Washington, Doug Fraser and UAW concessions, supplier and dealer pressure, K-car, minivan, and repayment narrative—core for 1979–83 industrial policy and labor.

Public voice and competition

Straight Talk / paperback materials: Japan, energy, industrial policy rhetoric, and celebrity CEO public role in the early Reagan years. Treat as Iacocca’s own framing as of the mid-1980s.
