Overview

Empire (1979; Norton 2004 reissue Howard Hughes: His Life & Madness) is Barlett and Steele’s four-year investigative biography of Howard Robard Hughes, Jr. (1905–1976), built from more than a quarter-million pages of scattered government, corporate, and court records rather than Hughes-company cooperation. The authors treat Hughes as a figure whose public legend—record aviator, Hollywood producer, defense contractor, reclusive billionaire—was sustained by secrecy, public relations, and decades of distorted repetition. The narrative runs from colonial Hughes family roots and Howard Hughes Sr.’s rock-bit invention through young Howard’s inheritance fight, Hollywood films (Hell’s Angels, Scarface), speed records and the 1938 around-the-world flight, wartime plywood bomber and flying-boat programs, the 1947 Brewster Senate hearings, and then postwar RKO, Hughes Aircraft’s electronics boom, TWA jet financing, breakdown and seclusion, Las Vegas empire-building, political influence, Watergate-era money trails, the Romaine Street burglary, CIA’s Glomar Explorer, death in Acapulco, and the Mormon-will fortune battle.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book covers Hughes’s postwar empire—RKO, Culver City electronics, TWA jets, Las Vegas, political machine, Watergate-era scandals, and CIA Glomar—through death and the fortune fight. Period-gate hard on Chs. 1–4 except as brief continuity; treat Ch. 5’s 1947 Senate/Spruce Goose as the opening hinge into the postwar narrative.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter / TOC

Lines 1–315. Also-by Barlett/Steele titles; Norton 2004 reissue copyright and original 1979 Empire title; Library of Congress data; full Contents listing Preface through Notes with chapter subheads (Family, Rock Eater, Hate Hughes Club, Project Jennifer, etc.).

Preface

Lines 316–448. Frames Hughes as the most misunderstood figure of his era; recounts 1976 death fingerprint verification for Treasury/FBI; contrasts public legend with documented losses and secrecy; describes four years of research across fifty cities, fifty government offices, and a fifty-thousand-page document collection—the largest outside the Hughes organization.

Acknowledgments

Lines 449–538. Credits anonymous Hughes employees and associates, dozens of libraries and newspaper archives, court clerks, and research collaborators who supplied access to the scattered record on which the biography rests.

Prologue — Death in Mexico

Lines 539–876. Opens on Hughes’s final hours at the Acapulco Princess Hotel in April 1976—Waldron pouring water, erratic breathing, heart stopping at 1:27 P.M.—then flashes back through the reclusive endgame that preceded his last flight home to Texas.

Chapter 1 — The Early Years

Lines 877–2456. Traces the Hughes clan from colonial Virginia through Missouri law and Iowa respectability; Howard Hughes Sr.’s Spindletop-era oil-field work and 1909 two-cone rock-bit patent founding Hughes Tool; Allene Gano’s courtship and Houston life; young Howard’s mother-dominated childhood; parents’ deaths; and the teenage fight with relatives and executives for control of the tool company and his inheritance.

Chapter 2 — Hollywood

Lines 2457–3141. Hughes and Ella Rice move to Los Angeles in 1925; failed Swell Hogan and modest Everybody’s Acting; Caddo Film Company and Noah Dietrich as administrator; obsessive production of Hell’s Angels with real aerial combat footage; and the censorship battles over Scarface that established Hughes as a producer willing to fight the Hays Office.

Chapter 3 — Hero

Lines 3142–4549. Aviation supplants movies after 1932; Hughes streamlines the H-1 racer at Lockheed, founds Hughes Aircraft as a tool-company division, sets speed records, and in 1938 completes the around-the-world flight in a Lockheed 14—cementing the Lindbergh-era hero image while rarely visiting Houston kin again.

Chapter 4 — The War Years

Lines 4550–6051. Hughes seeks Wright Field contracts against the perceived “Hate Hughes Club”; wins wartime bomber and flying-boat programs using duramold plywood construction; navigates strange procurement bedfellows and cost overruns; and pushes the giant Hercules flying boat and XF-11 reconnaissance prototype even as management chaos grows at Culver City.

Chapter 5 — The Senate Investigation

Lines 6052–7328. After Chuck Perelle’s reforms fail against Hughes’s interference, the July 1946 XF-11 crash in Beverly Hills triggers Senator Owen Brewster’s hearings; Hughes testifies in Washington while the Spruce Goose remains unfinished; the committee’s political theater culminates in Hughes flying the Hercules at Long Beach in November 1947—the hinge between wartime contracting and postwar empire.

Chapter 6 — Movies, Missiles, and Communists

Lines 7329–8556. Post-hearing staff reorganization brings Frank Gay to Romaine Street; Hughes buys RKO in 1948 and runs it from seclusion; anti-Communist crusading and blacklist politics mesh with his need to purge troublesome stockholders and reshape the studio.

Chapter 7 — Revolt at Culver City

Lines 8557–9569. Hughes Aircraft’s 1950s electronics and Falcon-missile boom outgrows Culver City management; Tucson plant tax controversy; a 1953 executive revolt over charity fronts, political insurance, and Hughes’s remote control of a now-massive defense contractor.

Chapter 8 — Seeds of Disaster

Lines 9570–10441. Mormon aides rise in the inner circle; Hughes commits to a $400-million TWA jet fleet order without clear financing; Rockefeller and Zeckendorf are drawn into a secret sale that never closes—planting the seeds of the late-1950s financial crisis.

Chapter 9 — Breakdown

Lines 10442–11671. Secret 1957 Tonopah marriage to Jean Peters; deepening isolation, psychiatric crisis, and institutionalization rumors; showdown with Noah Dietrich and Wall Street lenders over TWA jet commitments and Hughes Tool collateral.

Chapter 10 — Retreat

Lines 11672–12984. December 1960 voting-trust agreement strips Hughes of TWA control; flight to Rancho Santa Fe with germ-phobia rituals; manhunt when he vanishes from the Beverly Hills Hotel; lenders take the airline while Hughes retreats from public business.

Chapter 11 — A New Career

Lines 12985–13954. Four-and-a-half-year seclusion ends with a train move to Boston’s Ritz-Carlton; medical-institute cover stories; transition planning through Bob Maheu toward a Nevada relocation and a new phase of hidden operations.

Chapter 12 — Lord of the Desert

Lines 13955–14963. 1967 Desert Inn purchase launches the Las Vegas casino-buying spree; public-relations blitz and masterplan for Strip dominance; Washington warnings about monopoly and hidden ownership as Hughes becomes “lord of the desert.”

Chapter 13 — Out of Control

Lines 14964–16515. Rival Kirk Kerkorian builds the International Hotel; Hughes lieutenants feud; conspiracy fears, press leaks, and the Boxcar nuclear-test shelter scheme expose how little Hughes actually controls his Nevada empire.

Chapter 14 — The $90-Million Mistake

Lines 16516–17308. Vietnam-era helicopter contract mispricing costs Hughes a fifth of his fortune; lobbying Lyndon Johnson; the “buy-in” and renegotiation fights that typify his postwar defense-industrial missteps.

Chapter 15 — The Billion-Dollar Deal

Lines 17309–18086. Hughes insists on “billionaire” press identity while secretly strapped; cornering TWA stock then selling out to become liquid—turning airline equity into cash for Las Vegas and Summa operations.

Chapter 16 — The Hijacking of Air West

Lines 18087–18918. 1970 hostile takeover of regional carrier Air West (Bonanza/Pacific/West Coast merger); squeeze tactics, overbooked holiday flights, and CAB/Washington rubber-stamping that Maheu and Raymond Cook botch—another Hughes airline adventure ending in federal scrutiny.

Chapter 17 — Gold Fever

Lines 18919–20196. 1967–68 precious-metals panic; Hughes buys hundreds of Nevada mining claims through Maheu; prospector parades, sting operations, and mining-stock swindles tied to the Hughes name and President’s brother subplot.

Chapter 18 — Turning Point

Lines 20197–21456. Jean Peters’s 1970 divorce announcement; Hughes ousted from effective control by Bill Gay, Chester Davis, and Nadine Henley; Summa Corporation hands change while Hughes remains a figurehead in exile.

Chapter 19 — The Political Connection

Lines 21457–22383. Two decades of IRS, CAB, Interior, and Pentagon favors; secret contributions and “I can buy any man” bravado; political machine that runs on the Hughes name regardless of who actually pulls levers.

Chapter 20 — Life in Exile

Lines 22384–23649. Clifford Irving hoax autobiography of 1971–72; Bahamas move; $350-million Howard Hughes Medical Institute giveaway; Hughes declared invalid as aides wall him off from the world.

Chapter 21 — Watergate

Lines 23650–24690. December 1973 flight to Freeport on Khashoggi’s plane; Hughes–Nixon money threads, Bebe Rebozo, and Larry O’Brien as “well-wired source”—connecting the reclusive billionaire to the scandal consuming Washington.

Chapter 22 — Hughesgate

Lines 24691–25540. June 1974 Romaine Street (“the Bastille”) burglary of Hughes archives; million-dollar ransom for stolen files linking Project Jennifer, Watergate, and Las Vegas operations.

Chapter 23 — The CIA’s Best-Kept Secret

Lines 25541–26252. Los Angeles Times leak of Hughes–CIA contract to raise Soviet submarine K-129; Glomar Explorer cover story; Mell Stewart problem and grand-jury aftermath of the stolen Romaine documents.

Chapter 24 — The Last Year

Lines 26253–27715. Hughes’s seventieth year contrasts profitable Hughes Aircraft with loss-ridden Summa; legacy reckoning; last fights among aides; Candyman and missing-will anxieties before the final move to Mexico.

Chapter 25 — The Fight for the Fortune

Lines 27716–29563. April 1976 body flown Houston to Methodist Hospital; autopsy and identity confirmation; cover-up of neglect; Mormon will surfaced in Las Vegas; fallout among heirs, executives, and courts over a billion-dollar estate.

Epilogue — A Summing Up

Lines 29564–30316. Argues Hughes’s legend outran his business acumen—passbook savings would have beat his empire-building; fame and control of image, not Midas touch, drove movies, aviation, and seclusion; public relations sustained the superman myth after repeated corporate failures.

Notes

Lines 30317–36061. Endnotes keyed to chapters; documentary citations from FBI, CIA, SEC, CAB, congressional hearings, court records, and newspaper archives supporting the narrative.
