Grand Improvisation

Author: Derek Leebaert

Working text: Digital MOBI via Anna’s Archive (hash 5a15aa457e91e45abcc51e66f9bb5bd3)

Overview

Grand Improvisation is a diplomatic and strategic history of Anglo-American relations from the end of World War II through the late 1950s. Leebaert’s central claim is that the familiar “American Century” succession story—Britain too weak to continue, America confidently assuming leadership of a U.S.-led international order—is a myth. Britain did not want out; the United States did not suddenly shed continental insularity. For roughly a dozen years the two powers improvised together and against each other—sterling and Marshall aid, Middle East oil and bases, NATO and rearmament, Korea and Indochina, Iran and Egypt, Churchill’s return and Eden’s Suez—until Washington’s late-1950s break amounted to a practical declaration of independence from British influence.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book is a dense early-period spine: Truman–Eisenhower foreign policy, the British Empire/Commonwealth as America’s “outer fortress,” the Korean War as the true push into world political-military power, and the Suez/Hungary/Sputnik hinge into explicit U.S. supremacy. Pre-1946 wartime baselines appear for continuity; post-1957 material is mostly Conclusion spill (later Cold War misunderstandings, China rise, NASA/Webb coda) and should be period-gated.

MOBI structure: Kindle front matter and piracy notice; dedication (Michelle Nyama Kingue / Angela Lindsay Kingue); epigraph from Katharine Lee Bates, “America the Beautiful”; Introduction; Parts I–V with Chapters 1–20; Conclusion; Notes (by chapter); Acknowledgments; Index; Also by / Note About the Author; Macmillan newsletter and copyright block; trailing Contents list.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter

Lines 1–59. Farrar, Straus and Giroux ebook boilerplate; personal-use / piracy notice; dedication; Bates epigraph (“Confirm thy soul in self-control…”).

Introduction

Lines 60–237. Attacks the baton-passing myth of British collapse and instant American leadership; argues Britain remained a historic military-imperial power covering a quarter of the world’s land and that the United States remained a self-contained continental island-state. Frames the book as a world without American “grand strategy,” every Washington move a desperate improvisation, ending with Sputnik-era dread and Kennedy’s struggle-for-supremacy rhetoric as the later drive that still shapes policy.

PART I — They Thought It Was Peace

Lines 238–246. Part divider (photo caption: FDR and Vice President-elect Truman, 1944).

Chapter 1 — The Three in 1945

Lines 247–1094. Opens with Nat Gubbins’s 1946 sketch of Britain mute in austerity, Russia enormous and suspicious, America a nervous girl with a hand grenade; Hitler’s death and European war’s end; refugee masses; the three victors’ self-images and early postwar dispositions of power, occupation, and peace expectations.

Chapter 2 — Keynes & Co.

Lines 1095–1943. Keynes epigraph on Britain’s refusal to surrender and the price to follow; U.S. wartime boom versus fear of a peace bust / Depression return; Truman reassurances after Japan’s surrender; Anglo-American financial negotiation, Lend-Lease abruptness, and the sterling–dollar settlement struggle that opens the economic half of the improvisation.

Chapter 3 — Entering the Middle East

Lines 1944–2827. Imperial fable of communal conflict as frame; U.S. and British stakes in Middle Eastern oil, bases, and local politics as Washington steps into a region long treated as British imperial ground.

Chapter 4 — False Starts

Lines 2828–3939. Bevin on shocking Americans into consciousness (Feb 1947); Marshall’s China mission failure (Nanjing to Hickam); U.S. democracy/aid experiments ridiculed in the Foreign Office; false starts in China and related early Cold War initiatives that do not yet produce coherent American world policy.

PART II — A Chilling World

Lines 3940–3950. Part divider (Marshall, Bevin, and Ambassador Lewis Douglas, 1947).

Chapter 5 — Warfare States

Lines 3951–4952. Yeager / sound-barrier vignette as American technological swagger; simultaneous 1947 sterling crisis talks (Snyder, Clayton, Yorkshire miners) and Pentagon staff talks on Anglo-American military cooperation—finance and warfare-state building as twin tracks.

Chapter 6 — Year of the Offensive

Lines 4953–5770. 1938 Fascist-offensive analogy applied to Stalin’s 1948 pressure on Czechoslovakia and Berlin; Bevin’s 22 January 1948 Commons speech as a riveting step toward a remade Western order; year of Soviet offensive and Western response.

Chapter 7 — Defending the West

Lines 5771–6767. Bevin in Berlin May 1949 wanting Europe “settled for a couple of hundred years”; U.S. realization that Empire and Commonwealth are vital to protecting non-Soviet Europe; political-economic union and military cohesion seen to depend on Britain; Kremlin denunciations of “Marshallizing Europe in the British manner”; NATO-era Western defense architecture.

Chapter 8 — The Outer Fortress: Protecting the Middle East, Africa, and Asia

Lines 6768–7595. Hubert Humphrey (Sept 1949) calling the British Empire America’s outer fortress; renewed talk of British “bankruptcy” and fear that imperial collapse would pull down European economies before NATO allies could rearm; Middle East, Africa, and Asia as the fortress perimeter.

PART III — Chips on the Table

Lines 7596–7604. Part divider (Truman with Treasury Secretary John Wesley Snyder, 1949).

Chapter 9 — Auditing an Empire

Lines 7605–8450. Harriman’s complaint that America cannot say “No” to Britain; Bevin’s September 1949 Washington mission through early Korean War; Acheson and Snyder scramble to inventory British Empire strengths and weaknesses amid years of collapse talk in editorials and on Capitol Hill.

Chapter 10 — Pivoting to Asia and into Vietnam

Lines 8451–9142. MacArthur’s later claim that America’s existence was entwined with Asia; late-1949 U.S. rescinding of Middle East force commitments as bitter surprise to Bevin and to Admiral Conolly in London; pivot of scarce American “shots” toward Asia and the Indochina/Vietnam problem.

Chapter 11 — War on the Rim

Lines 9143–10032. Chip Bohlen: Korea, not World War II, made the United States a world political-military power; June 1950 Oxford Union regret at U.S. dominance; outbreak and early course of the Korean War as the hinge that forces global U.S. military engagement.

Chapter 12 — Asia’s Three Fighting Fronts

Lines 10033–10899. Acheson 1951: not enough shots to scatter worldwide; Fortress America habits persist despite Korea spending surge and labor strikes delaying buildup; stalemate replaces Dunkirk-evacuation fear; Korea, Indochina, and related Asian fronts as simultaneous drains.

PART IV — The Ground Changes

Lines 10900–10908. Part divider (Acheson and Eden, 1952).

Chapter 13 — “Holding the Ring” in Iran and Egypt

Lines 10909–11727. Ambassador Grady on British expectation that America follow British Middle East policy; Korea’s casualty scale versus expanding global commitments; Iran and Egypt crises as Britain “holding the ring” while seeking U.S. strength for British aims.

Chapter 14 — Churchill Is Back

Lines 11728–12639. Churchill quoting Cromwell to Truman on being versus well-being; 26 October 1951 return to office; cabinet of older men at Hyde Park Gate; renewed personal Anglo-American summit diplomacy under a second Churchill premiership.

Chapter 15 — Stemming the Tide

Lines 12640–13446. Churchill ready to learn if not always to be taught; Oliver Franks’s claim that Americans never have grand strategies—only ad hoc decisions under domestic stress; 1952 efforts to stem Communist and imperial-tide problems without a settled U.S. design short of outright war; Malaya (Templer, herbicides) and Kenya/Mau Mau as British colonial wars on a different scale from Korea.

Chapter 16 — Coronations and Crises

Lines 13447–14353. Eisenhower on leadership as getting others to want what you want; Eden’s pre-election plea that Ike not appoint Dulles; coronation-year politics and overlapping Middle East / European crises as Eisenhower–Dulles diplomacy begins.

PART V — A New Supremacy

Lines 14354–14367. Part divider (Eisenhower and Churchill at Bermuda 1953; Dulles 1954).

Chapter 17 — The Break Points of 1954

Lines 14368–15296. Dulles on success as not facing the same problem twice; Eisenhower’s January 1954 State of the Union inventory (Korea halt, Indochina aid, Europe unity, Iran “victory,” Middle East impartial friendship); 1954 as a cluster of break points that test whether America yet holds strategic initiative.

Chapter 18 — Ordering Chaos

Lines 15297–16220. Churchill warning that U.S. isolation would doom Europe and Britain; mid-1954 to early-1956 surge of American wealth, population, Fortune 500 earnings, and resolve relative to the Empire; attempts to order post-Korea, European, and Middle Eastern chaos from a position of growing material supremacy.

Chapter 19 — No End of a Lesson

Lines 16221–17272. Dulles refusing to champion the status quo; New Year 1956 review (Baghdad Pact, West Germany in NATO); Suez crisis and the harsh education of British (and French) power against American opposition—no end of a lesson in who now sets limits.

Chapter 20 — What Happened Next

Lines 17273–18172. Eisenhower on the psychological advantage of putting “the thing” up without hysteria (Oct 1957); post-Suez and Hungary sense that America’s world place had shifted more than in the late 1940s; from improvising into NATO and failing to impose liberal trade on Britain, through Korea’s push to global power, toward Sputnik-era recognition that only Washington and Moscow compete at the highest level.

Conclusion

Lines 18173–18573. Avoids casual “Cold War” label; argues each continent/decade had its own cold war and that late-century myths about how the conflict ended are as flawed as early ones; Bevin and Churchill’s effort to dispel American insularity; China rise analogy; remembrance of forgotten operators (Snyder, Douglas, MacDonald) versus James Webb’s NASA/civil-rights/graduate-education legacy and the James Webb Space Telescope coda on science as America’s historic advantage.

Notes

Lines 18574–23264. Chapter-by-chapter endnotes (Introduction through Conclusion) with archival citations (FO 371, HSTL, DDEPL, RG 59, ADST, etc.).

Acknowledgments

Lines 23265–23375. Research and institutional thanks.

Index

Lines 23376–25926. Alphabetical index.

Back matter

Lines 25927–26208. Also by Derek Leebaert; A Note About the Author (Knowledge Exchange site); Macmillan newsletter signup; copyright page (© 2018 Leebaert; maps © Jeffrey L. Ward; First edition 2018; e-book ISBN 978-0-374-71406-2); residual footnote-style asterisks; trailing Contents list.

Coverage Guide

Myth-busting frame and 1945 baselines

Introduction + Ch. 1 set the wiki’s early Cold War interpretive problem: no clean baton pass; three victors, austerity, and American unreadiness.

Sterling, Marshall, and the economic improvisation

Ch. 2 and Parts of Chs. 5, 7–9 cover Keynes/settlement, 1947 crisis, Marshallizing Europe “in the British manner,” and empire audit—core for postwar political economy pages.

Middle East as contested imperial ground

Chs. 3, 8, 10 (retrenchment), 13 (Iran/Egypt), 16–19 (to Suez) are the densest Middle East sequence for 1946–57 entity/event work.

Western defense and NATO

Chs. 5–7 carry warfare-state cooperation, 1948 offensive year, Berlin, and defending the West—use beside Church Committee and Burns for institutional Cold War pages.

Asia pivot, Korea, Indochina

Chs. 10–12 (and Malaya material in Ch. 15) document the shot-scattering problem and Korea as world-power hinge—priority for Korean War and early Vietnam commitment pages.

Churchill–Eden–Eisenhower–Dulles endgame

Chs. 14–20 cover the second Churchill government through Suez, Hungary, and Sputnik-era supremacy—wiki core for 1951–57 diplomacy.

Conclusion (period gate)

Use sparingly for later Cold War / China / NASA echoes; do not let 1960s–2020s coda drive entity pages outside the book’s 1945–57 spine unless completing a story already opened inside the period.
