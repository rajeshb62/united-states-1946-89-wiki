Overview

My Years with General Motors is Alfred P. Sloan, Jr.’s first-person corporate history of GM, written from the vantage of twenty-three years as chief executive and forty-five years on the board and its committees. With Fortune editor John McDonald and Catharine Stevens—and consulting historian Alfred D. Chandler—Sloan reconstructs policy documents and managerial reasoning rather than a private life memoir. The book’s spine is organization and strategy: Durant’s opportunity versus Ford’s Model T philosophy; the 1920 collapse and Pierre S. du Pont rescue; the concept of decentralized divisions with coordinated control; product policy (a car for every purse and purpose); the copper-cooled engine failure as organizational lesson; committee coordination and financial controls; transformation of the mass market in the 1920s; annual model change, styling, dealers, and GMAC; overseas and nonautomotive lines; World War II and Cold War defense production; personnel, the UAW, and incentive compensation; and a closing statement on change and progress.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book covers GM management as Sloan presents it in the early 1960s: postwar dealer modernization, permanent defense contracting, seventeen years without an extended national strike after the 1945–46 ordeal, bonus/incentive philosophy, overseas scale circa 1962–63, and Sloan’s claim that his generation uniquely shaped the large corporation and the automobile’s transformation of American life. Pre-1946 chapters are essential continuity for how that order was built; mine wartime and postwar chapters (esp. 16, 18–24) as period core, with earlier chapters as institutional baseline beside Iacocca, Walton, and Welch.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter

Lines 1–77. TOC; title page (Sloan; edited by John McDonald with Catharine Stevens; Doubleday, Garden City, 1964).

Preface

Lines 78–192. Justifies GM story from Sloan’s policy focal point; credits McDonald (Fortune), Catharine Stevens, Alfred D. Chandler (MIT consulting historian), and Fortune/research collaborators (Seligman, Parker, Silberman, Walker Evans as picture editor, et al.).

Introduction

Lines 193–461. Progress of the world’s largest private industrial enterprise across the century and the earth; Chevrolet, Pontiac, Oldsmobile, Buick, Cadillac, GMC as roughly half U.S. passenger cars and trucks; frames engineering arts and modern corporation as the book’s subject.

Chapter 1 — The Great Opportunity—I

Lines 462–952. 1908 dual founding moment: Durant forms General Motors from Buick base; Ford announces Model T—two philosophies that will dominate successive industry periods.

Chapter 2 — The Great Opportunity—II

Lines 953–1868. Sloan’s entry path: born New Haven, 23 May 1875; father’s Bennett-Sloan wholesale move to New York/Brooklyn; Hyatt Roller Bearing and route into GM via United Motors / Durant orbit—personal bridge into the corporation.

Chapter 3 — Concept of the Organization

Lines 1869–2467. End-1920 crisis: vanished market, shut plants, high-priced inventory, cash shortage, confused product line; simultaneous external slump and internal management crisis demanding reorganization.

Chapter 4 — Product Policy and Its Origins

Lines 2468–2947. Need for a concept of the automobile business after 1908–10 and 1918–20 expansions; logical industry concept as competitive force; origins of GM’s graded product ladder.

Chapter 5 — The “Copper-Cooled” Engine

Lines 2948–3936. Painful departure from first principles for ~2½ years; copper-cooled engine program as organizational failure and lesson despite Executive Committee push and divisional resistance.

Chapter 6 — Stabilization

Lines 3937–4079. Spring 1923: Pierre S. du Pont resigns presidency; Sloan succeeds; end of first modern-corporation period; credit to du Pont for rescue and return of general stability after 1920–21.

Chapter 7 — Co-Ordination by Committee

Lines 4080–4726. Fall 1923 boom atmosphere (first four-million vehicle year prospect); reconciliation after copper-cooled conflict; committee coordination as discipline for the 1920s boom.

Chapter 8 — The Development of Financial Controls

Lines 4727–6036. Financial control coevolving with committee coordination from 1920 ruins; modern financial policies as major advance alongside organization and product policy.

Chapter 9 — Transformation of the Automobile Market

Lines 6037–6849. Mid-1920s: strategy known in the mind but little reflected in market results by end-1924; then transformation of demand and competitive structure through the decade.

Chapter 10 — Policy Creation

Lines 6850–7685. By 1929 market transformation essentially complete; Ford’s Model A as stubborn old concept vs. Chrysler’s GM-like policy; Ford’s nearly two million of five million 1929 units as splurge not trend.

Chapter 11 — Financial Growth

Lines 7686–8624. GM as growth company; after 1918 and especially under modern administration, faster than industry to number-one producer; stakeholders—employees, shareholders, dealers, consumers, suppliers, government.

Chapter 12 — Evolution of the Automobile

Lines 8625–9399. Early reliability goal; motorists as risk-capital source; engineering evolution from buggy-like machines to modern cars.

Chapter 13 — The Annual Model Change

Lines 9400–9772. Annual models as accepted American life; vast management effort behind them; contrast with foreign/special cars; competitive advanced engineering and styling each year.

Chapter 14 — The Technical Staffs

Lines 9773–10424. GM as engineering organization (~19,000 engineers/scientists; 17,000 in divisions, 2,000 on general technical staffs); continuous technological progress as policy.

Chapter 15 — Styling

Lines 10425–11030. Styling as organized staff activity pioneered by GM in late 1920s; continuous interaction with engineering since 1928 producing modern GM style.

Chapter 16 — Distribution and the Dealers

Lines 11031–11955. Turbulence when markets shift buyers’/sellers’; novelty in each cycle; evolution of the dealer distribution system; postwar dealer facility and training-center expansion into the 1950s–60s.

Chapter 17 — GMAC

Lines 11956–12368. Why GM owns a major consumer-finance institution; GMAC’s share of U.S. car-credit; business sought only through GM dealers.

Chapter 18 — The Corporation Overseas

Lines 12369–13480. Free-world vehicle absorption and GM overseas position circa 1962–63; Overseas Operations Division assets/employees; manufacturing/assembly in twenty-two countries.

Chapter 19 — Nonautomotive: Diesel Electric Locomotives, Appliances, Aviation

Lines 13481–14872. Diversification limits: durables connected with motors (~10% civilian sales); diesel locomotives, appliances, aviation engines, earth-moving; Durant-era expansion contrasted with later discipline.

Chapter 20 — Contributions to National Defense

Lines 14873–15435. Permanent defense establishment as fact of corporate life; GM defense sales $350–500 million/year (1959–62), ~3% of sales; wartime and Cold War production role.

Chapter 21 — Personnel and Labor Relations

Lines 15436–16097. More than seventeen years without extended national-issue strike as of writing; contrast with mid-1930s violence and 1945–46 postwar strike; labor peace without surrendering basic management responsibilities; inflation critique of contracts.

Chapter 22 — Incentive Compensation

Lines 16098–16986. Bonus Plan since 1918 as integral management philosophy; 1942 annual-report statement placing executives as if running their own businesses; incentive as progress and stability tool.

Chapter 23 — The Management: How It Works

Lines 16987–17239. Motivation (incentive) and opportunity (decentralization); thesis that good management reconciles centralization and decentralization.

Chapter 24 — Change and Progress

Lines 17240–17567. Unique historical opportunity of Sloan’s generation: new product (automobile) and new organization type (large-scale corporation); transformation of United States and world economy.

Illustrations / Footnotes

Lines 17568–17902. Picture captions (early roads, Mercedes, Flint, etc.); numbered footnotes supporting chapters (e.g., early GM presidents; Ethyl sale 1962; dealer training centers).

Coverage Guide

Founding rivalry and Sloan’s entry (continuity)

Chs. 1–2: Durant vs. Ford Model T philosophies; Hyatt/United Motors path into GM.

1920 crisis, organization, product policy (continuity → institutional baseline)

Chs. 3–7: reorganization, product ladder, copper-cooled failure, du Pont→Sloan succession, committee coordination—how the modern GM was built.

Financial controls, 1920s market, growth (continuity)

Chs. 8–11: control systems, market transformation, 1929 competitive map, financial growth to industry leadership.

Product, styling, distribution machine (bridge + wiki)

Chs. 12–17: engineering evolution, annual model, technical staffs, styling, dealers, GMAC—operating system still governing postwar Detroit; Ch. 16 dealer modernization is strong 1946–89 material.

Overseas, diversification, defense, labor, management creed (wiki core)

Chs. 18–24: 1960s overseas scale, nonautomotive lines, Cold War defense work, post-1945–46 labor peace, bonus plan, decentralization thesis, generational coda—primary pages for this wiki beside Iacocca’s later Ford/Chrysler story.
