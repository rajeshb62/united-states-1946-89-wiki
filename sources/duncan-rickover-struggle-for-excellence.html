Overview

Rickover: The Struggle for Excellence is Francis Duncan’s full-life biography of Adm. Hyman G. Rickover (1900–1986), written by the Atomic Energy Commission / Naval Reactors historian who earlier co-authored Nuclear Navy, 1946–1962 (with Richard G. Hewlett, 1974) and wrote Rickover and the Nuclear Navy: The Discipline of Technology (1990). Duncan draws on Rickover’s papers (held by Eleonore B. Rickover), interviews, Naval Reactors access, presidential libraries, and official records. Eleonore Bednowicz Rickover’s foreword frames the private man behind the “father of the nuclear Navy” public image.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book is a primary spine for naval nuclear propulsion and Cold War fleet technology: pressurized-water reactor path, Nautilus, Shippingport civilian plant, Polaris and attack-submarine expansion, nuclear-carrier fights with McNamara, Thresher aftermath, congressional two-year extensions through 1982, shipbuilding-claims wars, Carter’s Naval Reactors connection, Three Mile Island testimony, and the engineering-discipline legacy.

Book structure: Plunkett Lake catalog front matter; Contents; Foreword (Eleonore B. Rickover); Preface; Chronology; Introduction; Chapters 1–23; Sources essay; Abbreviations Used in Notes; numbered endnotes.

Front matter / catalog / dedication

Contents

Lines 227–290. Foreword through Abbreviations Used in Notes; chapter titles 1–23 as listed below.

Foreword (Eleonore Bednowicz Rickover)

Lines 291–385. Nurse Corps path to Bethesda Tower 16 VIP ward; meeting hospitalized Rickover; January 1974 marriage (he newly four-star); Sanskrit “softer than flowers / louder than thunder” quotation; last letter at sea before 30 January 1982 retirement on personal responsibility; Maków and Holocaust awareness; endorsement of Duncan’s concise factual narrative.

Preface

Lines 386–489. Duncan’s 1969 AEC assignment with Hewlett to Naval Reactors; Nuclear Navy then Discipline of Technology; this volume as personal/career biography complementing the program histories; access, interviews, and purpose (engineer, education advocate, defense-program leader, philosopher of personal responsibility in a technical age).

Chronology

Lines 490–619. Capsule timeline: Jan. 1900 Maków birth; 1906 America / 1911 Chicago; 1918–22 Academy; destroyer La Vallette and battleship Nevada; Columbia M.S. electrical engineering 1929; submarines S-9 / S-48; New Mexico; EDO and China mine-sweeper command; BuEng/BuShips electrical section WWII; Dec. 1945 Western Sea Frontier mothballing then Oak Ridge; dual AEC–Navy hat; Nautilus / Shippingport; promotions and gold medals; Nixon USSR trip 1959; Thresher 1963; two-year extensions; Carter; Jan. 1982 retirement; July 1986 death in Arlington, Virginia.

Introduction — Forever Skeptical, yet Questioning

Lines 620–733. Opens on 10 July 1986 private Arlington burial (Eleonore, DeMars, Jewish earth tradition, taps); frames Rickover’s lifelong skepticism and the address “Thoughts on Man’s Purpose in Life” (“Our minds must be forever skeptical, yet questioning”); preview of National Cathedral memorial.

Chapter 1 — Becoming an Officer

Lines 734–1392. Birth date confusion (1 vs. 27 Jan. 1900); Maków, Russian Poland; Abraham and Rachel; New York then Chicago; John Marshall High School; Academy appointment; plebe struggle, anti-Semitism, and decision to make the Navy a life.

Chapter 2 — Sea Duty, Shore Duty, and Love

Lines 1393–2005. Ensign voyage to West Coast; destroyer La Vallette; battleship Nevada; postgraduate path; courtship correspondence with Ruth D. Masters; self-approval and nonconformity themes in letters.

Chapter 3 — Submarines and Marriage

Lines 2006–2967. S-9 and New London submarine school; S-48 duty; 1931 marriage to Ruth Masters; Inspector of Naval Material Philadelphia; Karl Compton “Utilizing of Atomic Energy” note as distant foreshadow; departure for New Mexico.

Chapter 4 — Engineering Duty Only

Lines 2968–3817. Assistant engineer on battleship New Mexico (1935–); Engineering Duty Only (EDO) decision; Asiatic station and mine-sweeper command; return to Bureau of Engineering on the eve of war; Ruth’s passage from Europe.

Chapter 5 — The Electrical Section and the War

Lines 3818–4808. August 1939 Bureau of Engineering arrival; Connecticut Avenue apartment; Electrical Section leadership in Bureau of Ships; wartime plant visits, equipment standards, and Pacific inspection trip ending Okinawa November 1945.

Chapter 6 — Atomic Energy

Lines 4809–5831. Dec. 1945 Western Sea Frontier mothballing duty; Ross Gunn “atom power for ships” testimony; path to Oak Ridge Naval Group; dual AEC–Navy organization; pressurized-water reactor choice; Mark I Idaho prototype authorization (1 July 1950); Cold War frame after Soviet bomb and Korea.

Chapter 7 — Promotion

Lines 5832–6536. Mark I and Nautilus January 1955 target; selection-board fights and engineer-officer hostility; congressional/press pressure; 24 July 1953 announcement—Rickover first on the rear-admiral list.

Chapter 8 — Nautilus

Lines 6537–6958. Mark I power operation June 1953; Bettis and Idaho crew training; Electric Boat construction; sea trials and 17 January 1955 “underway on nuclear power”; noise/vibration problems; NATO exercises proving submerged high-speed operations had changed naval warfare.

Chapter 9 — Shippingport Atomic Power Station

Lines 6959–7383. Parallel AEC civilian power project; carrier feasibility study under CNO Sherman; Shippingport pressurized-water plant on the Ohio River as engineering showpiece and foreign-dignitary stop; Sputnik-era context for atomic-power prestige.

Chapter 10 — A National Figure

Lines 7384–8179. Post-Sputnik defense and Anglo-American nuclear ties; Eisenhower pointing to atomic submarines; 1959 Nixon trip to the USSR—Lenin icebreaker inspection confrontation; education speeches and congressional witness role; play The Captain and the Kings; Gold Medal ceremony context.

Chapter 11 — The Program at the End of 1960

Lines 8180–8387. AEC year-end snapshot: Polaris boats at sea; Sargo and Seadragon Arctic voyages; surface-ship nuclear-propulsion cost-vs-advantage debate handed to the incoming Kennedy administration.

Chapter 12 — Breakthrough on Nuclear Carriers

Lines 8388–9007. 1960 election (Nixon admiration vs. Kennedy); McNamara and nuclear-carrier politics; long fight for nuclear attack carriers and escorts; later Nimitz two-reactor compromise forced by McNamara’s refusal of a four-reactor plant.

Chapter 13 — Meeting with Kennedy

Lines 9008–9171. 11 February 1963 White House meeting on NATO multilateral force (MLF); Rickover’s skepticism of mixed-manned nuclear surface force; education-standards interest cut short by assassination.

Chapter 14 — Thresher

Lines 9172–9403. 10 April 1963 loss of Thresher (129 dead); Rickover and Joint Committee scrutiny; SUBSAFE and quality reforms; Holifield’s remark that safeguards followed tragic loss of life.

Chapter 15 — Every Two Years

Lines 9404–9786. Mandatory retirement at sixty-four (Jan. 1964) vs. secretary recall; Joint Committee backing; birth of the recurring two-year extension that kept Rickover in charge until January 1982.

Chapter 16 — New Classes of Surface Ships and Submarines

Lines 9787–10452. Post-1964 extensions; McNamara nuclear-carrier agreement after Bettis visit; Los Angeles (SSN 688) class fight; Rivers anecdote on a threatened Rickover court-martial.

Chapter 17 — Troubles, Sorrow, and Happiness

Lines 10453–11248. 1968 election and Nixon/Laird; program and personal strains; Ruth’s decline and death; January 1974 marriage to Eleonore Bednowicz.

Chapter 18 — The Bitter Battle: Shipbuilding Claims

Lines 11249–11983. Late-1960s–1970s inflation and Vietnam-era cost surge; Todd settlement as opening; Rickover’s decade-long campaign against unsubstantiated/fraudulent claims; enmity that became a lever to force him out; contract-discipline ethic.

Chapter 19 — The Admiral and the President

Lines 11984–12891. Jimmy Carter’s Academy interview memory (“Why not?”); Carter presidency and Naval Reactors relationship; Three Mile Island era; 1979 two-year renewal carrying Rickover to January 1982 and the Reagan administration.

Chapter 20 — End of a Career

Lines 12892–13732. Reagan transition; age eighty-one still at long hours; decision not to renew; 1982 retirement ceremonies and succession (Kinnaird R. McKee / Executive Order framing joint Navy–Energy directorship); continuity of Naval Reactors discipline after his departure.

Chapter 21 — Death

Lines 13733–14234. Congress-mandated post-retirement office and advisory role; GPU/TMI-1 management assessments (1983–84); declining health; death July 1986; obituaries on technological revolution and demand for excellence.

Chapter 22 — Engineering Legacy

Lines 14235–14463. Reluctance to reduce principles to a “recipe”; post–Three Mile Island House testimony; 20 August 1979 “Principles of the Naval Nuclear Propulsion Program” memo; shop-floor presence, personal responsibility, and training pipeline as lasting legacy.

Chapter 23 — In Memoriam

Lines 14464–14572. 14 July 1986 Washington National Cathedral memorial; Carter reading Milton’s “On His Blindness”; tributes; Rickover Hall at the Naval Academy and the luck-rubbed bronze bust.
