Overview

Vision: A Saga of the Sky is Harold Mansfield’s thematic company saga of Boeing—not a formal corporate history but a narrative built on scores of interviews, records, and rechecks with participants (research assisted by Reynolds Phillips of Boeing Magazine). Mansfield centers engineering progress and individual vision (Egtvedt, Johnson, Allen, Wells, Schairer, Beall, Eddie Allen) rather than manufacturing detail. The frame is engineer John Haberlin preparing a speech at a 1950s thirty-year-awards banquet, looking back forty years from fabric-covered machines to the 707 jet and pilotless-aircraft work.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book is the principal near-contemporary Boeing jet-age account: postwar design leap, Stratocruiser bet, B-47/B-52, tanker-transport bridge to the 707, missiles/Bomarc, and Bill Allen–era commercial jet decisions. Period-gate Books One–Three to continuity except where needed to complete a postwar story.

Book structure: Foreword; Book One — THE CHALLENGE (10 chs.); Book Two — THE INSPIRATION (10 chs.); Book Three — THE TEST (8 chs.); Book Four — THE REALIZATION (11 chs.); Book Five — THE OUTLOOK (5 chs.); airplane timeline plates; illustrations lists.

Foreword

Book One — THE CHALLENGE (lines 307–3326)

Chapter 1 — Just Forty Years?

Lines 312–397. Opens in the 1950s with Haberlin watching a 707 fly over Seattle while assigned to pilotless-aircraft work; his banquet-speech assignment triggers a forty-year lookback at aviation’s acceleration from ~70 mph to jet speeds.

Chapter 2 — Meant to Fly

Lines 398–809. Bill Boeing at Dominguez Field (1910), thwarted by Louis Paulhan and Wright patent litigation; Westervelt partnership; early Seattle flying; Wong’s Model C stability innovations; Herb Munter’s test flights on Lake Union; Navy interest as America nears WWI entry.

Chapter 3 — Learning Things

Lines 810–1067. Wong applies Eiffel wind-tunnel thinking to Model C inherent stability; Navy school-plane competition; Munter’s control disputes; first flight November 1916; growing wartime production pressure.

Chapter 4 — Out of the Storm Cellar

Lines 1068–1319. Model C trials at Pensacola—Munter’s high-angle low-speed technique, rough-water tests; Navy acceptance; Boeing’s reputation as a serious designer amid fragile early aviation.

Chapter 5 — Production Needed

Lines 1320–1679. Armistice letter: Navy may halve HS-2L orders; Boeing urges flying-boat commercial development and side furniture/fixture work; B-1 flying boat; Vancouver airmail flight March 1919; postwar slump and payroll strain.

Chapter 6 — Airplanes, Not Cement Sidewalks

Lines ~1680–1841 (approximate). Egtvedt’s plea to keep engineering alive (“airplanes, not cement sidewalks”); Boeing backs R&D; Billy Mitchell’s GAX armored attack triplane; McCook Field contracts; MB-3A pursuit order brings production holiday to Seattle shops.

Chapter 7 — The Pursuit

Lines 1842–2564 (Ch. 8 Trials overlaps this band in OCR). MB-3A license production vs. original design frustration; metal-wood joint experiments and arc welding; PW-9 pursuit lineage; Navy fighter competitions; Haberlin’s crossed-wire fix wins forty-plane order.

Chapter 8 — Trials

Lines ~2183–2564 (OCR fuzzy—digest from TOC and pursuit/mail thread). NB trainer spin crises and Mitscher flight tests; tail-surface redesign battles with the Navy; production backlog and reputation risk before 1925 reorganization.

Chapter 9 — Much to Be Discovered

Lines 2565–2856. Boeing resumes presidency; Egtvedt chief engineer; PW-9C combat competition; Wasp engine breakthrough; experimental organization under Monteith; auto-rotation/spin research culture.

Chapter 10 — Flying the Mail

Lines 2857–3326. Hubbard’s Chicago–San Francisco airmail bid; Model 40 mail plane; Boeing Air Transport; Admiral “Bull” Reeves’s carrier-deck lecture on battleship vs. airplane striking power—stimulus for bigger, better aircraft.

Book Two — THE INSPIRATION (lines 3327–6600)

Chapter 1 — The All-Metal Idea

Lines ~3354–3717. Egtvedt visits Admiral Reeves (1928); Eddie Hubbard’s hotel-room sketch for an all-metal low-wing monoplane mail plane; Monteith’s cantilever/flutter fears; commitment to smooth monocoque skin.

Chapter 2 — Bomber Experiment

Lines 3718–4134. European tour (Dornier Do-X); Egtvedt launches Boeing bomber design; B-9 monoplane bomber; Martin B-10 competition politics; lessons on enclosed cockpits, speed, and Army procurement.

Chapter 3 — Why Not a Twin-engine Transport?

Lines ~4135–4568. Model 247 lineage after B-9 loss to Martin; United Air Lines pressure; February 1933 first flight—“they’ll never build ’em any bigger”; Mansfield’s “diminishing returns” vs. progress theme.

Chapter 4 — Project A

Lines ~4429/4569–4780. XB-15 “Project A” long-range bomber—30-ton, 5,000-mile ambition; Pratt/Wright Field politics; parallel four-engine transport studies; engineering of passageways, turrets, and unprecedented scale.

Chapter 5 — Flying Fortress

Lines 4781–5058. Model 299 (B-17 prototype) nonstop Seattle–Dayton flight; Wright Field competition vs. B-12/B-18; October 1935 crash from locked control surfaces; Army buys thirteen YB-17s despite disaster.

Chapter 6 — The Transoceanic Step

Lines 5059–5965. Beall’s dining-table Clipper sketches from XB-15 wings; Pan American 314 contract; Eddie Allen’s transoceanic flight tests; June 1939 Clipper first flight amid press and engineering drama.

Chapters 7–9 — Over the Weather / Running Ahead / Extremity

Lines ~5220–6440 (grouped; OCR chapter breaks fuzzy). Army GHQ bombardment procurement debates over B-17 after Dayton crash; XB-15 as symbol of “running ahead”; Stratoliner pressurization; Clipper weight/CG and sea-handling extremes before proving long-range flying boats.

Chapter 10 — Lightning Strikes

Lines 6441–6600. Eddie Allen proposes permanent combined flight-test and wind-tunnel research department reporting outside line engineering; Model 341 superbomber mock-up tube crawl; Phil Johnson’s RFC refinancing and Plant Two planning as war looms in Europe.

Book Three — THE TEST (lines 6601–9708)

Chapter 1 — Mounting Pressure

Lines 6613–~6775. Phil Johnson returns; RFC loan and stock issue wipe deficit; B-17 option fights with War Department; French Douglas-bomber license; mounting European crisis; “superbomber” word enters public imagination (1939).

Chapter 2 — Superbomber Crisis

Lines ~6776–7393 (no clean OCR chapter head; flows from Mounting Pressure). Model 345 submission; Blitzkrieg accelerates requirements; Echols demands B-17E armament; B-29 contract and 990-plane schedule; XB-29 structure and pressurization engineering.

Chapter 3 — War

Lines ~7394–7876. December 7, 1941; 19th Bombardment Group in Philippines—Kelly, O’Donnell, Wheless Fortress actions; retreat through Java and Australia; Rangoon raids; B-29 as distant hope; Eddie Allen flight-test group under wartime strain.

Chapter 4 — Around the Clock

Lines 7877–8290. Eddie Allen’s collaborative flight-test philosophy; 8th Air Force buildup; Ira Eaker to England; Knudsen production surge; Fortress modification for combat; shift to around-the-clock Seattle output.

Chapter 5 — Red Streak Program

Lines 8291–~8515. Summer 1943 8th Air Force offensive; August 17, 1943 Regensburg–Schweinfurt double mission planning; LeMay leads; “Flying Fortress against Fortress Europe.”

Chapter 6 — Fortress Against Fortress

Lines ~8516–8881. Deep-penetration losses (16% on August 17); Schweinfurt/Regensburg results; bomber-escort math; continuing Eaker/Anderson pressure to hit German aircraft industry.

Chapter 7 — Battle of Kansas

Lines 8882–~9299. B-29 engine and field-modification crisis; Salina/Pratt modification centers; first China-based raids on Japan; typhoon ditching lessons; Hap Arnold’s urgency to make Superfortress combat-ready.

Chapter 8 — Victory

Lines ~9300–9708. LeMay’s low-altitude incendiary gamble over Tokyo; production transition from B-17 to B-29 lines; XC-97 flight previews postwar transport; Beall’s Pacific tour; Bill Allen’s presidency notes; war’s end and reconversion anxiety.

Book Four — THE REALIZATION (lines 9709–13702; period gate → 1946–89)

Opening / jet-bomber studies / swept wing (Ch. 1 area)

Lines 9709–~9921. Post-VJ-Day jet and turboprop bomber studies; compressibility/Mach tunnel work; Phase One contract; Schairer–Tsien transatlantic slide-rule session discovers swept-wing drag advantage (May 1945).

Chapter 2 — Bet on the Stratocruiser

Lines 9922–~10174. September 1945 board meeting; Allen elected president; B-29 cutbacks vs. Model 377 Stratocruiser commercial bet; peacetime employment cliff.

Chapters 3–9 — B-47 through tankers (grouped; OCR titles fuzzy)

Lines ~10175–12864. XB-47 Stratojet development and first flight; range/compressibility dilemmas; Wichita production struggles (“second Battle of Kansas”); XB-52/YB-52; KC-97/KC-135 tanker competition; Allen’s price-first sales philosophy; Soviet threat driving jet bomber schedules.

Chapter 10 — The Case for the 707

Lines 12865–~13268. April 1952 board unanimously approves jet transport prototype; tanker-transport crossover design; B-52 first flight and 707 business case meeting (Barton, Pennell, Schairer, Wood).

Chapter 11 — Missiles and Nuclear Weapons

Lines 13269–13702. Post-707 prototype organization; Bomarc/GAPA missile lineage; nuclear-age defense framing; engineering shift toward electronics and materials.

Book Five — THE OUTLOOK (lines 13703–~14780; period gate → 1946–89)

Chapter 1 — Air Force First

Lines 13724–~13944. “Under God” Pledge of Allegiance episode; Allen defers commercial jet sales until KC-135 tanker decision; military production priority over Stratoliner.

Chapter 2 — Bomarc on the Track

Lines 13945–~14136. Bomarc ramjet test success; Wood’s vision of automated air defense; telemetry and H-bomb deterrence context.

Chapter 3 — The Open Mind

Lines 14137–~14183. Allen’s reverse-pricing for 707; Renton plant evidence for parallel tanker/commercial production; engineers on titanium, rockets, and outer-space horizons.

Chapter 4 — Progress and Principle

Lines 14580–~14592 (OCR jumps; section spans ~14184–14579). Peacetime competition as source of great Boeing designs; LeMay on SAC deterrence; Wells on moon-shot feasibility with funding and unity.

Chapter 5 — Vision

Lines ~14593–end plates. Haberlin’s banquet speech—challenge/inspiration/test/realization/outlook cycle; tension between technical progress and human peace; closing timeline plates and library stamp.
