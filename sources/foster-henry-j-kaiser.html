Overview

Henry J. Kaiser: Builder in the Modern American West is Mark S. Foster’s narrative biography of Henry J. Kaiser (1882–1967), drawing on the Henry J. Kaiser Papers, Edgar F. Kaiser, Sr., Papers, and Six Companies Papers at the Bancroft Library, presidential libraries, National Archives, oral histories with Kaiser executives and family, and Foster’s own prior articles on Kaiser’s regional industrialization, Detroit automobiles, and consumer/suburban culture. The book traces Kaiser from Steinheim, Germany, immigrant roots and Sprout Brook/Whitesboro childhood through Lake Placid and Florida photography chains, Spokane hardware and Hill Company paving apprenticeship, the 1914 Vancouver road-contract launch, 1920s California highway empire and Six Companies dam consortium, WWII Liberty-ship celebrity, western steel and magnesium, postwar Kaiser-Frazer and aluminum ventures, Kaiser Permanente medical care, Hawaii resort development, and the dispersal of Kaiser Industries after his death.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book covers Kaiser’s postwar empire: Kaiser-Frazer’s Detroit challenge, overseas engineering, aluminum, Permanente HMO scale-up, Hawaii Kai and Pacific ventures, management succession, death in 1967, and corporate breakup. Period-gate hard on dams/ships/WWII chapters except as brief continuity.

Book structure: Foreword; Acknowledgments; Introduction; Chapters 1–16; Conclusion; Notes; Bibliographic Note; Index.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter / Contents

Lines 1–148. American Studies Series imprint; title page and copyright © 1989 University of Texas Press; Library of Congress and ebook ISBN data; reprint permissions for Foster’s prior Business History Review, Michigan History, and Western Historical Quarterly articles; contents listing Foreword through Conclusion, Notes, Bibliographic Note, and Index.

Foreword (William H. Goetzmann)

Lines 149–254. Goetzmann frames Kaiser for interdisciplinary American Studies: WWII Liberty-ship legend, Horatio Alger camera-marketing origins, dams, steel, labor sympathy, and Kaiser Permanente; compares Kaiser’s Washington navigation and western maverick style to Lyndon Johnson; argues Kaiser continued the federal-aid tradition of western development from Topographical Engineers through reclamation and tourism.

Acknowledgments

Lines 255–416. Foster credits University of Colorado Denver fellowship support, Edgar F. Kaiser, Jr.’s research grant and manuscript reading, Bancroft and National Archives staffs, Hoover/Roosevelt/Truman/Eisenhower libraries, dozens of former Kaiser executives interviewed, Eugene E. Trefethen, Jr., Kaiser family members, and his wife Rickey Lynn Hendricks (whose Kaiser health-plan dissertation informed the project).

Introduction

Lines 417–610. Opens on Kaiser’s July 1942 Portland cargo-plane speech, Oval Office visit, War Production Board “runaround,” and Howard Hughes collaboration as a frame for asking who Kaiser was; surveys his West Coast infrastructure and wartime industries; discusses rival irritation, government partnership, postwar Permanente and labor stances; and states Foster’s biographical method (entrepreneurial “seat-of-the-pants” operator rather than Chandler-style organizational synthesis).

Chapter 1 — Roots

Lines 611–1017. Henry John Kaiser born 9 May 1882 at Sprout Brook, New York; parents Frank and Mary (née Yops/Jobst) as German immigrants from Steinheim; Frank’s cobbler trade, family instability, and 1889 move to Whitesboro on the Erie Canal; Henry’s gregarious childhood, eighth-grade departure from school, Utica dry-goods and photography apprenticeship, mother Mary’s 1899 death, and 1901 departure for Lake Placid.

Chapter 2 — Launching a Career

Lines 1018–1606. Photography partnership with W. W. Brownell at Lake Placid (1901–), Florida chain expansion (Daytona, Jacksonville, St. Augustine, Palm Beach), courtship and 1907 marriage to Bess Fosburgh, Spokane McGowan Brothers hardware sales, Hawkeye Fuel and J. F. Hill paving apprenticeship, 1913 Hill Company management shakeup and Kaiser’s integrity finishing contracts; closes with 1914 firing and readiness to strike out as an independent contractor (Oakland HQ horizon).

Chapter 3 — Taming the Wilderness—Roads

Lines 1607–2318. Kaiser launches general construction in Vancouver, B.C., 1914—$25,000 Commerce Bank loan, Ordway hire, Warren Brothers backing, 1916 Federal Aid Road Act shift to rural Washington/Oregon/Idaho jobs, 1921 Redding–Red Bluff U.S. 99 contract and Oakland headquarters, Le Tourneau earth-moving partnership, Philbrook and Livermore gravel plants, Gas Tax Act lobbying, 1927–1930 Cuba Central Highway subcontract, and transition toward dam bidding as Depression arrives.

Chapter 4 — Taming the Wilderness—Dams

Lines 2319–3459. Depression contraction pushes Kaiser toward federal mega-projects; Colorado River reclamation politics and Black Canyon (Hoover/Boulder Dam) bidding; formation of Six Companies consortium; construction conditions at Boulder City; Grand Coulee and related Columbia Basin work; cement, aggregates, and Washington lobbying through Harold Ickes and New Deal agencies.

Chapter 5 — Patriot in Pinstripes—Shipbuilding

Lines 3460–4513. Kaiser’s lifelong boating interests; 1937–39 entry via Seattle-Tacoma Shipbuilding and Todd partnership on C-1 cargo ships; wartime Richmond, Portland, and Vancouver yards; Liberty-ship prefabrication, labor relations, and record launch times that made Kaiser a national symbol.

Chapter 6 — Man of Steel

Lines 4514–5552. Steel-plate shortages for shipyards drive Kaiser toward a fully integrated western mill; Big Steel resistance, Morgenthau and OPM/Gano Dunn capacity debates; Fontana (Kaiser Steel) authorization, labor, and wartime plate production.

Chapter 7 — Creating an Image

Lines 5553–6242. Kaiser’s rise from obscure contractor to Pearl Harbor–era celebrity; early Elks and AGC service; 1931 Hoover Dam press fights; Washington “point man” role under Hoover and Roosevelt; Corcoran and Ickes connections; wartime press, “Miracle Man” coverage, and 1944 vice-presidential speculation.

Chapter 8 — Planning for a Postwar World

Lines 6243–6935. Kaiser’s future-oriented temperament; December 1942 NAM address urging reconversion planning; housing, health care, industrial diversification, and magnesium/aluminum peacetime visions amid rival executives’ skepticism.

Chapter 9 — Debacle in Detroit

Lines 6936–8001. Kaiser’s wartime automobile interest (Fuller Dymaxion, hobby-lobby tinkering); August 1945 Kaiser-Frazer partnership with Joseph W. Frazer; Willow Run and Detroit production; market reception, dealer network, UAW relations, and decline of the auto venture.

Chapter 10 — Venturing Abroad

Lines 8002–8632. Debunks the “Cuba bribery ended overseas work” myth; Permanente cement Pacific bulk shipments and Panama/Hawaii wartime supply; 1943 Latin America scouting; 1954 Argentina Perón automobile deal and broader postwar international cement, engineering, and assembly investments.

Chapter 11 — Cargo Planes and Government Investigations

Lines 8633–9443. July 1942 five-thousand-plane proposal; Hughes partnership and HK-1/H-4 “Spruce Goose” prototypes; War Production Board politics; postwar C-119/C-123 programs; 1947 congressional investigation of Hughes–Kaiser spending.

Chapter 12 — Light Metals—Heavy Profits

Lines 9444–10119. 1941 western aluminum-fabrication proposals blocked by Alcoa; Ickes support; magnesium plants (Hansgirg carbothermic process, Permanente and Baton Rouge); postwar Kaiser Aluminum expansion and profitability.

Chapter 13 — Kaiser and the Doctors

Lines 10120–11175. Prepaid industrial medicine precedents; workers’ compensation and lodge practice; Dr. Sidney R. Garfield on Colorado River Aqueduct; 1938 Grand Coulee prepaid plan; WWII Richmond and Portland shipyard clinics; 1945 public Permanente launch and AMA “socialized medicine” opposition.

Chapter 14 — Boss

Lines 11176–11877. Kaiser’s consistent hands-on management from 1914; Ordway, Sherwood, and field supervision; loyalty to key men, equipment innovation, long hours, and delegation patterns across construction, dams, shipyards, and later industries.

Chapter 15 — Global Development and a Pacific Paradise

Lines 11878–12869. Kaiser Engineers domestic and overseas work; India Tata Iron contract; Argentina and Brazil automobile plants; 1954 Hawaii “retirement,” tourism, hotels, broadcasting, and planned community development.

Chapter 16 — The Sunset Years

Lines 12870–13324. Kaiser’s sociability and work-centered life; Lake Tahoe Fleur du Lac, powerboating, and rare recreation; Hawaii years; Bess’s death; second marriage to Alyce Chester; declining health; death 24 August 1967 at Honolulu.

Conclusion

Lines 13325–13578. Dispersal of Kaiser Industries after 1967; Edgar Kaiser’s stewardship versus Henry’s inspirational drive; foreign competition, energy crisis, and sale of steel, aluminum, and other lines; Kaiser as last of the personality-driven “captains of industry” and as a government-partnered western developer.

Notes

Lines 13579–17034. Chapter-by-chapter endnotes citing Bancroft Kaiser collections, federal and state records, newspapers, interviews, and Foster’s prior articles.

Bibliographic Note

Lines 17035–17276. Foster’s research narrative: 1981 decision to write the first Kaiser biography; New York Times and periodical-index chronology; Bancroft Papers (~700 linear feet) and gaps in intimate correspondence; presidential libraries, corporate archives, and specialized collections (Reuther UAW papers, Haughey shipyard letters, Weinerman Permanente files, etc.).

Index

Lines 17277–19373. Name and subject index (aircraft, Alcoa, AMA, automobiles/Kaiser-Frazer, Bechtel, dams, Hawaii, labor, Permanente, shipbuilding, steel, and related entries).
