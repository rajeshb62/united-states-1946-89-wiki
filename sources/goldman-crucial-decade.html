Overview

The Crucial Decade—and After is Goldman’s contemporary narrative survey of American public life from the Japanese surrender in August 1945 to the edge of the 1960 election. The original Knopf volume ran through the 1955 Geneva summit; the Vintage edition adds two chapters and an epilogue carrying the story through Sputnik, the Middle East and Berlin crises, the kitchen debate, Camp David, the U-2 collapse, and late-Eisenhower popularity amid elite unease. Goldman’s thesis is that 1945–55 forced the country to answer two linked questions—whether to accept ongoing international responsibility and whether to keep the half-century of domestic economic-social reform—and that after near double-negatives the nation answered both in the affirmative, then lived inside that “Eisenhower equilibrium.”

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book is a dense primary-adjacent scholarly narrative for the Truman–Eisenhower core: reconversion and strikes, Vandenberg internationalism, Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan politics, Clifford and policy planning, China and the Soviet bomb, Hiss/Acheson and the naming of McCarthyism, Korea and MacArthur, 1952 realignment, massive retaliation and domestic conservatism without full rollback of the New Deal, Army–McCarthy, highways and soil bank, and the bumpy late-1950s middle road.

Narrative Source Flow

Preface to the Vintage Edition

Lines 71–249. Explains the 1956 Knopf Crucial Decade (1945–Geneva 1955) and the Vintage extension through 1960; defends 1945–55 as a definable, uniquely “crucial” epoch in which Americans worried toward decisive affirmatives on internationalism and domestic reform; catalogs period memories (atomic bomb, Jackie Robinson, Hiss, Korea/Changjin, “We like IKE,” McCarthy “point of order,” Eisenhower middle-roadism); points to the end-matter source note.

Chapter 1 — Mood Maybe

Lines 250–910. V-J announcement in the Oval Office (August 14, 1945) and nationwide celebration; boom-and-bust dread, atomic doomsday unease, and “would history repeat the last postwar?”; conservative anti–New Deal / anti-communist mood sketches (including De Voto on universities); the suspended, ambivalent “maybe” temper of late 1945.

Chapter 2 — Strikes, Russians, and Harry Truman

Lines 911–2983. Truman’s plow-for-gun desk symbolism and Jackson County “simple” ethic; reconversion strikes and domestic turmoil; early Soviet friction; Vandenberg’s wartime conversion from isolationism (London buzz-bombs to 1945 Senate renunciation); Eightieth Congress counter-revolution mood—cuts, resisted housing/controls/social security/education, immigration nativism—as “atmosphere you had forgotten.”

Chapter 3 — The Postwar Takes Shape

Lines 2984–3259. Foreign crisis overruns Taft’s domestic agenda: British withdrawal from Greece (February 1947), Turkey/eastern Mediterranean stakes, Marshall’s “Battle of the Bulge” analogy; White House legislative rush; Acheson reframes the pitch away from WPA-style relief toward strategic stakes for Republican leaders; 1946 election paradoxically frees Truman to be himself rather than FDR’s executor.

Chapter 4 — Containment, Foreign and Domestic

Lines 3260–4638. Tightening White House authority (“Harry Truman is no pushover”); Clark Clifford’s rise; Policy Planning / Kennan economic-aid logic and limits (healthy societies, China judged unsalvageable by aid under Chiang); 1948 election defying Taft’s social world; containment abroad paired with containing Taftism at home amid Jackie Robinson–era social upsurge.

Chapter 5 — Year of Shocks

Lines 4639–5924. 1949 inaugural parade and “Hi, Harry” popularity; fall of China; Soviet atomic explosion years ahead of scientific expectation (Urey: only thing worse than one nation having the bomb is two); blame attaching to Roosevelt–Truman handling of Communism; maxim that the party credited for sun also gets the rain.

Chapter 6 — The Great Conspiracy

Lines 5925–6682. Fury over Communist advances fused with middle-aged experience of breakneck domestic change (welfare state, status jostling, “harum-scarum” standards); State Department as perennial suspect (Henry Adams quoted); Hiss case atmosphere and the search for conspiracy explanations of foreign-policy failure.

Chapter 7 — Dinner at the Colony

Lines 6683–7231. Acheson’s “I do not intend to turn my back on Alger Hiss” (Matthew 25) and its political cost in 1950; Joe McCarthy’s early ruthlessness and college-election anecdote; Herblock (Block) cartoon labeling the tar barrel “McCARTHYISM,” fixing the name of the revolt born of 1949 shocks.

Chapter 8 — Suspended Moment

Lines 7232–8583. Saturday–Sunday June 24–25, 1950: Truman in Independence, Acheson at Harewood, UN figures weekend-scattered; UP flash on North Korean attack; failed Seoul call; Blair House dinner and decision circle; occupation-soft Eighth Army and slow reinforcement; military economizing under Truman and Louis Johnson as precondition of unreadiness.

Chapter 9 — The Hills of Korea

Lines 8584–10053. Early war collapse—Dean’s capture, Remp and GI retreat narratives, “fighting with a feather”; training and segregation practice at Fort Jackson (McConnell uses Gray directive to end local segregation without asking permission); MacArthur prestige (DAR tribute) rising amid the grinding hill war.

Chapter 10 — Yearnings and the Fulfillment

Lines 10054–11806. MacArthur’s April 19, 1951 address to Congress—thirty interruptions of applause, “I could not answer” on denying military advantages; Senate hearings and Republican policy statement calling dismissal reasons “utterly inadequate” and Far East policy a “desolate failure”; 1952 yearning for a fulfilling leader; George Humphrey’s businessman path toward Treasury and the conservative turn in economic management.

Chapter 11 — No Fear of Conservatism

Lines 11807–12996. Humphrey at Treasury—“ask him if he has seen George?”—hiring freezes, fewer federal projects, end of Korean wage-price controls; Dulles massive retaliation and Radford “New Look” defense cuts versus Truman budgets; cultural untraditionalism (TV violence, I Love Lucy pregnancy) beside political conservatism without full restoration of pre–New Deal order.

Chapter 12 — The Eisenhower Equilibrium

Lines 12997–14340. 1954–55 relaxation that is not old isolationist “normalcy” but a worked-at peace under ongoing Communist pressure; Eisenhower “middle-of-the-road” after an early right-leaning phase; Army–McCarthy hearings climax (Welch–McCarthy exchange over Fisher); split government becoming habitual; parties distinguished by degree and nuance (Republican measures the jigger; Democrat pours).

Chapter 13 — More of the Same

Lines 14341–15838. 1955–57 execution of Crucial Decade decisions: Half-Century of Revolution inching via compromise—social-security extensions, public housing units, interstate highway user’s tax, soil bank after veto of rigid supports; Sputnik I as prestige/security shock (Jackson “devastating blow”; Hagerty “great scientific interest”); path toward summit diplomacy; Nixon Moscow visit and exhibition tour setup.

Chapter 14 — The Middle Road Grows Bumpy

Lines 15839–16390. Kitchen debate (Nixon–Khrushchev on washing machines vs. rockets and ultimatums); Camp David and removal of Berlin ultimatum precondition for Big Four meeting; Eisenhower’s 22,000-mile prestige tour; return of Cold War tone from Moscow/Peiping; warning against complacent hot-dog summitry and bungling.

Epilogue

Lines 16391–16657. Post–U-2 Gallup still ~68% for Eisenhower while elite/intellectual anxiety deepens after Sputnik; partisan and nonpartisan critics (Lippmann, Life, Times, Rockefeller); critics not rejecting the two great Crucial Decade decisions (international responsibility; reformed mixed economy) but doubting leadership quality; closes on the nation’s capacity to meet further crucial decades.

Note on the Sources and Acknowledgments

Lines 16658–16774. Method: ~printed mass plus selected manuscripts, interviews, and post-draft participant fact-checking (~85% response); hope to deposit correspondence at Princeton; named helpers (Acheson, Kennan, Austin, Rickey, Stevenson, Urey, and many others); Bennett Hill, Fredrick Aandahl, Princeton Research Fund, Knopf staff.

Index

Lines 16775–end. Subject index (Acheson, containment, atomic bomb, McCarthy, Korea, etc.) and residual Vintage/back-cover blurbs; OCR quality poor in places.

Coverage Guide

Reconversion, Truman persona, and the Eightieth Congress

Chapters 1–3 for V-J mood, strikes, Truman’s self-presentation, Vandenberg’s conversion, and the 1946–47 domestic counter-revolution meeting the Greek-Turkish crisis.

Containment doctrine and 1948 politics

Chapter 4 for Clifford, Policy Planning limits (including China), Truman Doctrine/Marshall politics, and the 1948 social coalition that beat Taftism.

1949 shocks through the naming of McCarthyism

Chapters 5–7 for China, Soviet bomb, conspiracy temper, Hiss/Acheson, Colony dinner, and Herblock’s “McCarthyism.”

Korea, MacArthur, and the 1952 fulfillment

Chapters 8–10 for Blair House decisions, early war failure, desegregation-in-practice vignette, MacArthur speech/hearings, and Eisenhower/Humphrey conservative managerial turn.

Eisenhower equilibrium and late-1950s bumps

Chapters 11–14 and Epilogue for New Look/massive retaliation, Army–McCarthy, middle-road domestic compromise, Sputnik, kitchen debate, Camp David to U-2, and the standing of the two “great decisions.”
