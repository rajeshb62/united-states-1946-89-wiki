Overview

Walt Disney: The Triumph of the American Imagination is Neal Gabler’s biography of Walt Disney (1901–1966), arguing that Disney reshaped American popular culture by turning animation into character-and-emotion art, inventing the theme park as total imaginative environment, and supplying audiences not merely escape but vicarious control over a crafted reality. The life arc runs from hard Elias Disney childhood and midwestern make-believe through Kansas City go-getter years, Hollywood Alice comedies and Mickey Mouse, Depression-era shorts cult and Snow White “folly,” Fantasia/feature Parnassus, the 1941 studio strike and World War II training/propaganda films, postwar creative drift (Song of the South and package features), Disneyland and ABC television as a “city on a hill,” and late EPCOT/utopia projects until death—capped by the cryogenics myth Gabler dismantles in the Introduction.

For this United States 1946–89 wiki the book is a core popular-culture and entertainment-industry spine: postwar studio recovery and live-action turn, Disneyland (1955) and Disney television, merchandising scale by 1966, Cold War–era “control” thesis, and EPCOT planning. Chapters One–Eight (birth through wartime) are retained for continuity; Nine–Eleven plus Introduction/Appendix are the wiki priority. Period-gate family/estate aftermath beyond Walt’s 1966 death.

Narrative Source Flow

Front matter

Lines 1–94. TOC; dedication to daughters; Blake epigraph (“I must create a system…”); chapter-illustration key (1915 drawing through 1966 EPCOT map).

Introduction

Lines 95–506. Cryogenics rumor after Disney’s death vs. cremation at Forest Lawn; 1966 audience/merchandising scale; Disney as reshaper of animation, Pop Art adjacency (Fantasia/Stokowski), recreation (Disneyland), and urban design—“Disneyfication” vs. Rouse/Blake praise.

Chapter One — Escape

Lines 507–2227. Elias Disney’s hard, devout temperament; Walt’s childhood need to escape into drawing and make-believe; formative midwestern family pressures.

Chapter Two — Go-Getter

Lines 2228–3600. Post–World War I return from France into booming America; job hunt and Kansas City commercial-art/animation hustle; youthful exuberance of the early 1920s.

Chapter Three — Wonderland

Lines 3601–5074. Move to Hollywood as spiritual destination; Alice comedies with Ub Iwerks; dress-up, attention, and the capital of imagination in the mid-1920s movie boom.

Chapter Four — The Mouse

Lines 5075–7553. Train home after Mintz betrayal; vow never to work for anyone again; creation and rise of Mickey Mouse; Lillian’s fear and Walt’s rage as founding myth of independence.

Chapter Five — The Cult

Lines 7554–9626. Depression immunity of Disney shorts relative to overextended studios; Three Little Pigs and the 1930s cult of Disney character animation.

Chapter Six — Folly

Lines 9627–12472. Restlessness after Three Little Pigs; decision on something big—the feature that becomes Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937), long dismissed as folly.

Chapter Seven — Parnassus

Lines 12473–15658. Post–Snow White peak months; grosses and studio triumph; drive toward Pinocchio, Fantasia, Bambi and the Burbank plant as artistic Parnassus—and financial strain beneath.

Chapter Eight — Two Wars

Lines 15659–18443. Belt-tightening under Roy; animators’ strike (1941); World War II takeover of studio capacity (Victory Through Air Power, training films); twin wars of labor and global conflict.

Chapter Nine — Adrift

Lines 18444–21386. Postwar wait to reclaim studio from soldiers and bankers; package features and Song of the South (1946); plans for Alice, Peter Pan, Mickey feature—creative drift and recovery struggle.

Chapter Ten — City on a Hill

Lines 21387–25060. Control thesis: Disney offered empowerment over crafted reality, not mere escape; Leuchtenberg’s “troubled feast” postwar mood; Disneyland planning (Herb Ryman/ABC) and television as new city on a hill.

Chapter Eleven — Slouching Toward Utopia

Lines 25061–28694. Disneyland-driven stock and revenue surge (1952–58); Wall Street formula of wringing value from every property; EPCOT and late utopian schemes; Walt’s final years and death (1966).

Appendix

Lines 28695–28882. Chronological list of feature-length pictures produced by Walt Disney (animation, live action, combination, True-Life).

Notes / Bibliography / Acknowledgments / Copyright

Lines 28883–41809. Dense chapter notes; selected bibliography; acknowledgments; author note / also by Gabler; Knopf 2006 copyright and LC cataloging (eISBN 978-0-307-26596-8).
